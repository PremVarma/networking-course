Load Balancers — Many Servers, One Front Door

In Part 5 you learned a hard rule: two Spring Boot instances cannot both bind to port 8080 on the same machine. In Part 7 you learned the liberating consequence: a stateless app can run as many identical copies as you like. This part is where those two facts collide and demand a new device — a single, stable front door that spreads incoming requests across a fleet of identical servers and quietly stops sending traffic to any one of them that dies. That device is the load balancer, and almost every production system you will ever touch sits behind one.

1. Why load balancers exist

Everything you have built so far in this course terminates at a single server process. In Part 1 you met the single point of failure — the mainframe whose death darkened every terminal in the building. In Part 5 you met the brick wall that says two processes cannot share one TCP port. In Part 7 you met the property that rescues you from both: statelessness enables horizontal scaling. The load balancer (LB) is the missing piece that turns those three facts into a working production architecture.

Start by being honest about the limits of one server. A single Spring Boot instance on a single machine has a finite amount of everything:

  • Finite CPU — it can only execute so many request handlers per second before threads queue.
  • Finite RAM — the JVM heap, the HikariCP connection pool, in-flight request buffers all share one fixed pool of memory.
  • Finite connections — there is a hard ceiling on simultaneous open sockets (the 5-tuple space and file-descriptor limits from Part 5).
  • Finite uptime — the process will eventually be killed: a deploy, an out-of-memory crash, a kernel panic, a hardware fault, or simply you running mvn spring-boot:run again with a new build.
🧭 Why this exists

A load balancer exists to solve two distinct problems at once — and the entire chapter depends on you keeping them separate in your head:

  1. SCALE — one server cannot serve all your users, so spread the incoming requests across many identical servers, multiplying your total capacity. This is about throughput: handling 50,000 requests per second (RPS) when one box tops out at 5,000.
  2. AVAILABILITY — any one server can die at any moment, so make sure the system survives losing one (or several) of them without users noticing. This is about resilience: surviving a crash, a bad deploy, or an entire data center going dark.

A load balancer is the in-path device that achieves both by sitting between clients and a pool of servers, sending each request to a server it believes is alive and not overloaded. Without it, you are back to one box: one ceiling, one failure domain.

These two goals are not the same thing, and conflating them is a classic interview trap (we return to it in the misconceptions). You can have scale without availability (ten servers behind a dumb splitter that keeps sending traffic to a crashed one) and availability without much scale (two servers, one active and one cold standby). A good load balancer gives you both, but they are earned by different features: scale comes from the distribution algorithm; availability comes from health checks. Keep that distinction; it organizes everything below.

🌍 Analogy — the receptionist at the front desk

Throughout this course we have used the postal system and a building's front desk. The load balancer is the receptionist. Visitors (clients) arrive at one well-known address — the building's front door — and never need to know which floor, desk, or staff member will actually help them. The receptionist looks at a board of who is available, who is on a break, and who called in sick, and routes each visitor to a staffer who can take them right now. If a staffer goes home, the receptionist simply stops sending visitors their way — no visitor is sent to an empty desk. If the queue grows, you hire more staff and tell the receptionist about them; visitors still walk through the same one door. The genius of the design is that the front door's address never changes no matter how the staff roster churns. That stable front door is the load balancer's whole reason for being.

2. What came before — and why it broke

📜 What came before

Before dedicated load balancers, engineers tried to spread traffic with the tools they already had. The earliest and most common trick was DNS round-robin (Part 6): you give one hostname several A records, and the DNS server hands them out in rotation, so different clients resolve api.example.com to different server IPs. It is free, requires no new hardware, and "balances" in the loosest sense. Before even that, shops did manual failover: a human (or a script watching a ping) would notice the primary server was down and repoint DNS or swap a cable to the standby. Both approaches were better than nothing and both were quietly broken in ways that bit operators at 3 a.m.

Let's be precise about why DNS round-robin is not a real load balancer, because understanding its failures is the cleanest way to understand what a load balancer must actually do. Recall from Part 6 that DNS answers are cached and governed by a TTL (Time To Live).

  1. No health awareness. DNS hands out .12 on its turn whether or not .12 is alive. It is a phone book, not a doctor — it has no idea a server crashed. One-third of your users get connection-refused errors until a human edits the zone.
  2. Cache stickiness. Once a client (or its resolver, or the OS, or the browser) caches .10, it keeps using .10 for the whole TTL — and many clients ignore TTL and cache far longer. Distribution is therefore coarse and sticky, not per-request.
  3. Uneven distribution. One corporate proxy or one mobile carrier resolving once and serving thousands of users behind it sends all of them to a single IP. "Round-robin" across resolvers is nothing like round-robin across users.
  4. Slow failover bounded by TTL. Even after you remove a dead server's record, clients keep the old answer until their cache expires. A 300-second TTL means up to five minutes of users hitting a corpse. Lower the TTL and you hammer DNS and still cannot go below seconds.
🔑 Key point

DNS round-robin distributes names to addresses, once, at lookup time, with no knowledge of server health and no control after the answer leaves. What you actually need is an in-path device that makes a fresh decision for every single connection, using live health data, and can react in seconds. That device is the load balancer. DNS still plays a role — it points clients at the load balancer's address — but it is the doorman's address book, not the doorman.

3. Horizontal vs vertical scaling

There are exactly two ways to make a system handle more load, and the choice between them dictates whether you even need a load balancer.

  • Vertical scaling (scale up) — make the one box bigger: more CPU cores, more RAM, faster disks. You upgrade your EC2 instance from an m5.large to an m5.24xlarge. Simple — your code does not change, there is nothing to coordinate — but it has a hard ceiling (there is a biggest box money can buy), it usually requires downtime to resize, and crucially it is still one failure domain. A huge server that crashes takes 100% of your traffic with it.
  • Horizontal scaling (scale out) — add more boxes: run three, ten, fifty identical instances and spread load across them. This needs two things: a load balancer in front, and stateless application instances (Part 7) so any instance can serve any request. The payoff is enormous: capacity scales nearly linearly (double the instances, roughly double the throughput), there is no single failure domain (lose one of fifty and you lose 2% of capacity, not 100%), and you can grow and shrink the fleet on demand (autoscaling).

🔑 Key point — cattle, not pets

Cloud-native design prefers horizontal scaling, and the culture has a one-liner for it: treat servers like cattle, not pets. A pet has a name, you nurse it back to health when it is sick, and losing it is a tragedy. Cattle are numbered, interchangeable, and if one is unwell you replace it without ceremony. Horizontal scaling only works if your instances are cattle — identical, disposable, stateless (Part 7) — which is exactly why the load balancer can pull a dead one out of rotation and slot a fresh one in without anyone noticing. Vertical scaling produces pets; horizontal scaling produces a herd plus a receptionist who manages it.

In practice you use both: scale the stateless app tier horizontally behind a load balancer, and scale the hard-to-shard database vertically (until it too needs sharding or read replicas, a Part 13 topic). The rest of this chapter is about the horizontal tier and the device that makes it possible.

🟢 Beginner — say it simply

You can make a system handle more work two ways: get a bigger computer (vertical) or get more computers (horizontal). More computers needs a "traffic director" in front — the load balancer — so users still reach one address while the work is shared. The advantage of more computers is that if one breaks, the others keep going.

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

Vertical scaling raises one node's resources; it is simple but ceiling-bound and remains a single failure domain. Horizontal scaling adds nodes behind a load balancer and scales capacity near-linearly while removing the single failure domain, but it requires statelessness (Part 7) so any node can serve any request — or a shared session store if you cannot avoid state. Autoscaling groups add and remove nodes from the LB's pool based on metrics like CPU or request count.

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

Horizontal scaling trades a capacity ceiling for coordination problems: you now own load distribution, health detection, graceful draining, session handling, and the "thundering herd" risks of a fleet reacting in unison (section 14). The economics also flip — commodity horizontal fleets ride Moore's-law pricing while the biggest vertical boxes carry a premium — but databases and other single-writer systems resist horizontal scaling without sharding or consensus, which is why the canonical pattern is "stateless tier scales out, stateful tier scales up until it must shard." Knowing which tier you are scaling is half the architecture interview.

4. The core mechanism — one VIP, many backends

Here is the heart of it. Clients connect to one stable address, and the load balancer that owns that address forwards each request to one of N servers behind it. That stable address is either a VIP (Virtual IP — an IP address that belongs to the load balancer rather than to any one backend) or, more commonly in the cloud, a DNS name that resolves to the load balancer. The pool of servers behind it goes by several interchangeable names you will see in different tools:

  • targets — AWS's word (a "target group");
  • upstreams — Nginx's and HAProxy's word (an "upstream block");
  • backend pool — the generic term, and what Azure calls it;
  • members or real servers — older hardware-LB vocabulary.

They all mean the same thing: the actual servers doing the work. Picture three identical Spring Boot instances, each listening on 8080, each on its own private IP inside your network (Part 3's 10.0.x.x range):

Notice three things that make this work and that we will keep returning to:

  1. The public address belongs to the LB, not the servers. The three Spring Boot instances sit on private IPs (Part 3) and are not directly reachable from the Internet. The world only ever sees 203.0.113.50. You can add a fourth instance, kill the second, redeploy the third — the front door is unchanged.
  2. The port problem from Part 5 dissolves. All three instances happily bind to 8080 because they are on different machines (different IPs), so the 5-tuple is unique for each. The LB then maps its own listener port (say 443) to whichever backend's 8080 it chose.
  3. Statelessness (Part 7) is what lets this be transparent. Because any instance can serve any request, the LB is free to pick a different backend for every request from the same user. If the app stuffed session data in instance #1's memory, request two landing on #2 would break — which is the sticky-session problem we revisit in section 11.
🔑 Key point — but isn't the LB itself a single point of failure?

Yes — and this is the most important caveat in the chapter. If everything funnels through one load balancer and that load balancer dies, you have simply moved the single point of failure, not removed it. The fix is that a real load balancer is never one box. It runs as a redundant HA (High Availability) pair — two LB nodes sharing the VIP, one taking over instantly if the other fails — or, in the cloud, as a managed, horizontally-scaled fleet. AWS runs an Application Load Balancer as a managed service spread across multiple Availability Zones (Part 11), automatically scaling its own capacity; you never see or run "the box." So the load balancer must itself be highly available, by the same logic that made you build it in the first place. We will hammer this point again in the misconceptions, because interviewers love the "isn't the LB a SPOF?" question.

🌍 Analogy — one published phone number, a bank of receptionists

A large company publishes a single phone number on its website. Internally, that number rings a bank of receptionists, and a switchboard routes your call to whichever one is free. You, the caller, never dial a specific receptionist's extension and never notice when one goes to lunch. And the switchboard itself is not a single fragile machine in a closet — it is duplicated, with a backup that takes over silently if the primary fails. The published number is the VIP; the receptionists are the backends; the switchboard is the highly-available load balancer.

🟢 Beginner — say it simply

Users connect to one address. Behind that address, the load balancer secretly forwards each request to one of several identical servers. Users never know how many servers there are or which one answered. And the load balancer is itself duplicated so it does not become the new weak point.

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

The LB owns a virtual IP (VIP) or DNS name and maintains a pool of backend targets on private IPs. For each incoming request it selects a healthy target by its algorithm and forwards traffic, mapping its listener port to the target's port. Backends bind the same port on different hosts, so the 5-tuple (Part 5) stays unique. The LB is deployed as an HA pair or managed multi-AZ fleet so it is not itself a single point of failure.

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

"One VIP, many backends" hides real choices: whether the LB terminates the connection (L7) or forwards it (L4), whether it preserves or rewrites the source IP (SNAT, section 6), how the VIP fails over (gratuitous ARP for an on-prem HA pair, or DNS/anycast for a managed fleet), and how state about which client maps to which backend is kept (or deliberately not kept). Managed cloud LBs additionally scale their own data plane, so the "single VIP" is often many physical nodes behind one anycast address resolved per-AZ — meaning the front door is itself a small distributed system.

5. Health checks — the feature that makes it more than a splitter

If a load balancer only spread requests evenly, it would be a glorified splitter — and would happily send one-third of your traffic to a crashed server, exactly like DNS round-robin. What elevates a load balancer to a real availability device is the health check: the LB continuously asks each backend "are you okay?" and routes only to the ones that say yes. This is the feature that delivers the AVAILABILITY goal from section 1.

5.1 Active vs passive checks

  • Active health checks — the LB proactively probes each backend on a schedule, e.g. an HTTP GET /actuator/health every 15 seconds. If the backend returns 200, it is healthy; if it times out or returns a non-2xx, a failure is recorded. After a configured number of consecutive failures (the unhealthy threshold), the LB stops routing to it; after a number of consecutive successes (the healthy threshold), it brings it back. This is the primary mechanism.
  • Passive health checks — the LB also watches real traffic. If a backend starts returning connection resets or 5xx errors on actual user requests, the LB can mark it suspect without waiting for the next active probe. Passive checks react faster to sudden failures but cannot, by themselves, tell when a server has recovered (no real traffic is being sent to it) — so they pair with active checks.

5.2 Shallow vs deep checks — a genuine trade-off

What the health endpoint actually tests is a real design decision with a sharp edge:

  • Shallow check — "is the process up and accepting connections?" This is a bare TCP connect, or an HTTP 200 from a trivial /health that returns {"status":"UP"} without touching any dependency. Cheap, fast, and it answers exactly one question: is the server listening?
  • Deep check — "is this server actually able to do useful work?" The health endpoint itself pings the database, checks the Redis connection, verifies it can reach a downstream service. Spring Boot Actuator does this out of the box: /actuator/health aggregates a db indicator (runs a validation query through HikariCP), a redis indicator, a diskSpace indicator, and more.
⚠️ The deep-check cascade — read this twice

Deep checks sound strictly better, but they have a famous failure mode. Suppose all ten instances share one database, and the database has a brief hiccup. Every instance's deep /actuator/health now returns DOWN because the DB indicator fails. The load balancer dutifully marks all ten healthy servers as unhealthy at once and pulls the entire fleet out of rotation — turning a recoverable 30-second database blip into a total outage, and possibly a cascading one as retries pile up. The lesson: a health check should report whether this instance is healthy, not whether a shared dependency is healthy. A common compromise is a "liveness" check that is shallow (am I up?) and a separate "readiness" check that is deeper (can I serve traffic?) but scoped carefully — a distinction Kubernetes formalizes and Part 12 explores.

5.3 Connection draining — graceful removal

What happens to requests already in flight when you take a backend out of rotation — say, during a deploy? If the LB kills the backend instantly, every request mid-flight is severed and users see errors. The fix is connection draining (AWS calls it deregistration delay): when a target is being removed, the LB stops sending it new requests but lets existing in-flight requests finish, up to a timeout (default 300 seconds on an ALB). Only then is the target fully removed.

🧪 Real example — graceful rolling deploy

This is exactly what makes zero-downtime rolling deployments and blue/green deployments work. To deploy a new version across three instances:

  1. Deregister instance #1 from the target group. The LB stops routing new requests to it but lets its current requests drain (connection draining).
  2. Once drained, deploy the new build to #1, wait for its /actuator/health to return 200 for three consecutive checks, then register it back.
  3. Repeat for #2, then #3. At no point are fewer than two healthy instances serving traffic, so users see zero downtime.

Blue/green does the same idea with two whole fleets: stand up a complete "green" fleet on the new version, health-check it, then swing the LB's traffic from "blue" to "green" — and swing it straight back if anything looks wrong. Health checks plus draining are the primitives that make both safe. On the application side, Spring Boot supports graceful shutdown (server.shutdown=graceful) so the JVM finishes in-flight requests and runs HikariCP's connection-cleanup callbacks before the process exits — the app's half of the same handshake.

🟢 Beginner — say it simply

The load balancer keeps asking every server "are you okay?" every few seconds. If a server stops answering, the LB stops sending it visitors until it recovers. When you deploy new code, the LB quietly stops sending new requests to a server, lets it finish what it is doing, updates it, and adds it back — so users never notice.

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

Active checks probe an endpoint (e.g. /actuator/health) on an interval, using healthy and unhealthy thresholds to debounce flapping; passive checks observe real-traffic errors. Shallow checks test only liveness; deep checks test dependencies but risk cascading the whole fleet down when a shared dependency blips. Connection draining (deregistration delay) lets in-flight requests finish before a target is removed, enabling rolling and blue/green deploys.

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

Health-check design is a reliability discipline: scope checks to per-instance health, separate liveness from readiness, and avoid coupling a server's health to a shared dependency's health to prevent correlated removal. Tune thresholds and intervals against your failover SLO versus false-positive flapping; account for the probe load itself on the fleet. Draining timeouts must exceed your longest legitimate request but not so long that bad instances linger. Mature setups also guard against "fail-open vs fail-closed" — what should the LB do if every target reports unhealthy? Some LBs fail open (route anyway) on the theory that a fleet-wide unhealthy signal usually means a bad check, not a dead fleet.

6. Layer 4 load balancing

📍 OSI Layer 4 — Transport

Now the distinction this entire chapter lives on. Load balancers come in two flavors depending on which OSI layer (Part 2) they operate at, and the difference determines what they can and cannot see — and therefore what they can and cannot do.

A Layer 4 load balancer (L4 LB) operates at the transport layer (Part 4): it works with TCP and UDP. The critical fact is what it can see. An L4 LB looks only at the 5-tuple from Part 5 — source IP, source port, destination IP, destination port, and protocol — the addressing on the "envelope." It does not open the envelope. It has no idea whether the bytes inside are an HTTP GET, a SQL query, a Redis command, or a gRPC call. To an L4 LB, a connection is just a flow of bytes between two endpoints to be forwarded.

6.1 Two modes — NAT and Direct Server Return

  • Connection / NAT mode — the most common. The LB rewrites the destination IP+port of each packet from the VIP to a chosen backend (destination NAT, from Part 3), and the return traffic flows back through the LB. Depending on the topology, it may also rewrite the source address — called SNAT (Source Network Address Translation) — so the backend's reply is guaranteed to return via the LB. SNAT is convenient but it hides the real client IP from the backend (the backend sees the LB's IP), which is the seed of the X-Forwarded-For problem in section 9.
  • Direct Server Return (DSR) — an optimization where inbound requests pass through the LB but backends reply directly to the client, bypassing the LB on the (usually larger) return path. This lets the LB handle enormous traffic because it only touches the small inbound half. It is fiddly to set up and less common in the cloud, but worth knowing the term.

6.2 The defining strengths and limits of L4

Strengths. Because an L4 LB does almost nothing — read five fields, rewrite an address, forward — it is extremely fast and adds negligible latency. It is also protocol-agnostic: since it never inspects the payload, it works for any TCP or UDP traffic. PostgreSQL on 5432, Redis on 6379, Kafka on 9092, raw gRPC, a custom binary protocol, plain non-HTTP TCP — an L4 LB balances them all identically, because to it they are just bytes. And because it does not terminate TLS, TLS passes straight through it untouched (TLS passthrough, Part 8): the client's encrypted session runs end-to-end to the backend, which holds the certificate and decrypts. The LB never sees plaintext and never needs the cert.

Limits. The very blindness that makes it fast makes it dumb about content. An L4 LB cannot route by URL path (it cannot see the path), cannot read or inject HTTP headers, cannot do cookie-based routing, cannot inject a stickiness cookie, cannot terminate TLS to inspect or modify requests. If you want /api to go to one pool and /images to another, an L4 LB simply cannot do it — that information lives inside the envelope it refuses to open.

🌍 Analogy — the mail sorter who only reads the address label

An L4 load balancer is a mail sorter who reads only the address on the outside of a sealed envelope and drops it into the right delivery bin. Fast, works for any kind of letter — a bill, a love letter, a legal notice — because the sorter never opens anything. But ask that sorter to "send all the invoices to the finance bin," and they are helpless: figuring out it is an invoice would require opening and reading the letter, which is precisely what they never do. That is L4 in one image.

🟢 Beginner — say it simply

A Layer 4 load balancer only looks at the "to" and "from" addresses on each packet — not what is inside. That makes it very fast and able to balance any kind of traffic (databases, caches, anything), but it cannot make decisions based on web addresses (URLs) because it never reads them.

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

An L4 LB forwards TCP/UDP flows using only the 5-tuple, via NAT (rewriting destination, optionally source via SNAT) or Direct Server Return. It is protocol-agnostic and low-latency, and passes TLS through to the backend untouched, so the backend terminates and the LB never sees plaintext. It cannot route by path, header, or cookie, and cannot inject anything, because it does not parse the application payload.

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

L4 balancing operates on flows, so its "stickiness" is naturally per-connection; cross-connection affinity needs 5-tuple or source-IP hashing. SNAT exhausts ephemeral ports under very high connection counts (a real scaling limit, Part 5), and source-IP preservation versus SNAT is a routing-symmetry trade-off. Because TLS is opaque, an L4 LB cannot do SNI-based routing unless it peeks at the unencrypted ClientHello SNI field (TLS SNI inspection) — a hybrid trick NLBs and HAProxy support without fully terminating. DSR scales the LB by removing it from the response path entirely.

7. Layer 7 load balancing

📍 OSI Layer 7 — Application (HTTP)

A Layer 7 load balancer (L7 LB) operates at the application layer (Part 2/7). Unlike L4, it terminates the connection — it completes the TCP handshake (Part 4) and the TLS handshake (Part 8) with the client itself, decrypts the bytes, and reads the full HTTP request. Now, instead of seeing an opaque flow, it sees everything Part 7 taught you: the method (GET, POST), the path (/api/orders), the Host header, every other header, and the cookies. That visibility unlocks a long list of capabilities an L4 LB cannot touch.

   │ pool        │      │ pool        │        │ pool        │
   └─────────────┘      └─────────────┘        └─────────────┘

Because it reads the request, an L7 LB can do all of the following:

  • Content / path-based routing — /api/* to service A, /images/* to service B, based on the URL path.
  • Host-based routing — Host: admin.example.com to the admin pool, Host: shop.example.com to the storefront pool, all behind one IP.
  • Header- and cookie-based routing — route X-Canary: true requests to the new build, or route by a tenant ID header.
  • Sticky sessions via an injected cookie — this is precisely where the Part 7 sticky-session problem is actually solved. The L7 LB can set its own cookie (e.g. AWS's AWSALB) so the same client keeps returning to the same backend. (Section 11 explains why you usually should not want this.)
  • TLS termination — the certificate lives on the LB (Part 8). The LB decrypts, and either forwards plaintext HTTP to backends on a trusted private network, or re-encrypts to the backends (TLS re-encryption / end-to-end TLS).
  • Request rewriting, redirects, compression, response caching — rewrite paths, issue HTTP→HTTPS redirects, gzip responses, cache static responses.
  • Rate limiting and WAF integration — throttle abusive clients and plug into a WAF (Web Application Firewall) that inspects requests for SQL injection, XSS, and other attacks — only possible because the LB can read the request.
🔑 Key point — the cost of L7

None of this is free. An L7 LB must parse HTTP for every request, must hold and manage the TLS certificate and private key, must do the cryptographic work of terminating TLS, and is therefore slightly slower and more resource-hungry than an L4 LB. And it is HTTP-aware only — an L7 HTTP load balancer cannot balance raw PostgreSQL or Kafka traffic, because those are not HTTP. You buy intelligence with CPU and protocol specificity. That is the fundamental L4-vs-L7 trade, and the next section makes it a table.

🌍 Analogy — the receptionist who reads your form

If L4 is the mail sorter reading only the address, L7 is the front-desk receptionist who opens your intake form, reads "I'm here for a dental appointment," and walks you to the dental wing — and who can also check your ID at the door (WAF), tell you the dental wing is full and ask you to wait (rate limiting), and stamp your form with a number so you see the same dentist next visit (sticky cookie). They can do all this only because they read the form. The price is that reading every form takes longer than glancing at an address, and a receptionist trained to read dental forms is useless for sorting raw shipping containers (non-HTTP traffic).

🟢 Beginner — say it simply

A Layer 7 load balancer actually opens and reads the web request, so it knows the URL, the website name, and the cookies. That lets it send /api requests to one group of servers and /images to another, handle HTTPS for you, block bad requests, and more. The catch: reading everything is a bit slower, and it only understands web (HTTP) traffic.

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

An L7 LB terminates TCP and TLS, parses the HTTP request, and routes on path, host, header, or cookie. It can inject stickiness cookies, terminate or re-encrypt TLS (cert lives on the LB, Part 8), rewrite/redirect, compress, cache, rate-limit, and integrate a WAF. The cost is HTTP parsing, cert management, and TLS CPU per request, plus it is HTTP-only — it cannot balance arbitrary TCP protocols.

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

Because the L7 LB terminates the connection, it owns connection pooling and keep-alive to backends independently of client connections, enabling HTTP/2 and HTTP/1.1 multiplexing, request coalescing, and retry-on-idempotent semantics. It becomes the natural place for cross-cutting concerns (authn offload, header normalization, observability), which is exactly why an L7 LB blurs into a reverse proxy (Part 10) and an API gateway. The trade is that it is now a stateful, CPU-bound, HTTP-semantics-bearing component on the critical path — and the place where most subtle production bugs (header handling, timeouts, buffering) live.

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

L4 forwards flows on the 5-tuple, is protocol-agnostic, low-latency, and passes TLS through. L7 terminates and parses HTTP, enabling content routing, TLS offload, stickiness, rewriting, and WAF — at the cost of CPU and HTTP-only support. Pick L7 for HTTP microservice routing and offload; pick L4 for non-HTTP, ultra-high throughput, static IPs, or end-to-end TLS.

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

The decision is rarely binary in large systems: a layered topology (L4 edge for anycast static IPs and DDoS absorption, L7 behind it for routing) is common, as is pushing L7 logic into a service mesh sidecar (Part 13). The deeper L4-vs-L7 axis is where TLS terminates and who owns HTTP semantics — which dictates where you can do authn, observability, and request mutation, and where the cert and plaintext live. Choosing L4 to keep TLS end-to-end is often a compliance or zero-trust requirement, not a performance one.

9. The X-Forwarded-For problem

This is the section backend engineers hit in production constantly, so slow down here. The moment an L7 load balancer terminates the client's connection (section 7) and opens its own connection to your backend, a subtle and infuriating thing happens: your Spring Boot app no longer sees the real client. From the app's point of view, every request arrives from the load balancer's IP, because the LB is the one that actually connected to it.

Fig 9.6 — Why the backend sees the LB's IP, not the client's
   real client                 LOAD BALANCER (L7)            Spring Boot
   203.0.113.7                 10.0.1.5                      10.0.1.11
       │                            │                            │
       │  TCP+TLS connection #1     │                            │
       │ ─────────────────────────► │  terminates here           │
       │  src = 203.0.113.7         │                            │
       │                            │   NEW connection #2         │
       │                            │ ─────────────────────────►  │
       │                            │   src = 10.0.1.5  ◄──────── app's
       │                            │  (the LB's own IP!)          getRemoteAddr()
       │                            │                              returns 10.0.1.5

If your app naively logs request.getRemoteAddr(), every log line says 10.0.1.5 — the LB — and you have lost the real client IP entirely. Worse, if you rate-limit by remote address, you are now rate-limiting the load balancer, so one abusive client trips the limit for everyone. The solution, which you already met in Part 7, is a set of X-Forwarded-* headers the LB injects before forwarding:

# What an L7 load balancer adds to the request before forwarding to your app:

X-Forwarded-For:   203.0.113.7              # the real client IP (the one that connected to the LB)
X-Forwarded-Proto: https                    # the client spoke HTTPS to the LB (even though LB→app is plain http)
X-Forwarded-Host:  shop.example.com         # the Host the client originally requested
X-Forwarded-Port:  443

# If there are multiple proxies in the chain, X-Forwarded-For becomes a comma-separated list,
# left = original client, each hop appends the IP it received the request from:
X-Forwarded-For:   203.0.113.7, 198.51.100.4, 10.0.0.9
#                  ^client      ^outer proxy   ^inner proxy

For L4 load balancers, which never parse HTTP and so cannot inject HTTP headers, the equivalent is the PROXY protocol — a small header prepended to the TCP stream that carries the original source IP and port. The backend (or the L7 LB sitting behind the L4 LB) must be configured to read it.

9.1 The redirect-loop trap — getScheme() returns http

Here is the specific bug that has cost more engineers an afternoon than almost any other in this course. When an L7 LB terminates TLS (section 7) and forwards plain HTTP to your backend on a private network, your Spring Boot app sees an http request — even though the user is on HTTPS. So request.getScheme() returns "http", request.isSecure() returns false, and any code that says "if this request is not secure, redirect to the HTTPS version" fires — sending the browser to https://..., which the LB terminates and forwards as http again, which triggers the redirect again. You have built an infinite redirect loop, and the browser gives up with ERR_TOO_MANY_REDIRECTS.

🧪 Real example — fixing it in Spring Boot

The fix is to tell Spring to trust and honor the forwarded headers, so it reconstructs the original scheme, host, and client IP from X-Forwarded-* instead of from the LB connection it actually sees:

# application.properties — tell Spring Boot it is behind a proxy
server.forward-headers-strategy=framework
#   FRAMEWORK = Spring's built-in ForwardedHeaderFilter (recommended for ALB / Nginx)
#   NATIVE    = let the servlet container (Tomcat) handle it (good for some PaaS like Cloud Foundry)
#   NONE      = ignore the headers (the default — and the cause of the redirect loop)

# After enabling, behind a TLS-terminating ALB:
request.getScheme()      // now correctly returns "https"
request.isSecure()       // now correctly returns true
request.getRemoteAddr()  // now resolves to the X-Forwarded-For client IP, 203.0.113.7

With framework set, getScheme() returns https, the redirect condition is false, the loop never starts, and your logs and rate limiter see the real client IP again.

⚠️ Security caveat — never blindly trust these headers

X-Forwarded-For is just a header, and anyone can send it. If a client connects to you directly and sends X-Forwarded-For: 1.2.3.4, a naive app will believe its IP is 1.2.3.4 — perfect for bypassing an IP allowlist or framing another address in your logs. The rule: only trust X-Forwarded-For from your own load balancer. Configure the set of trusted proxy IPs/ranges, and take the client IP as the rightmost entry that is not one of your own proxies (or the leftmost, depending on your trust model and how many hops you control). Trusting the header unconditionally is a real, exploited vulnerability — not a theoretical one.

🟢 Beginner — say it simply

Behind a load balancer, your app sees the load balancer's address, not the real visitor's. The LB writes the real visitor's address into a special header called X-Forwarded-For. You must tell your app to read that header — otherwise your logs are useless and you can accidentally create an endless HTTPS redirect loop.

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

Connection termination replaces the client's source IP with the LB's, so getRemoteAddr() returns the LB. The LB preserves the original IP, protocol, and host in X-Forwarded-For/-Proto/-Host (or PROXY protocol for L4). Set server.forward-headers-strategy so Spring reconstructs scheme/host/IP; without it, TLS-terminated http requests cause getScheme()=="http" and HTTPS redirect loops. Trust the headers only from known proxy IPs.

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

Forwarded-header handling is a trust-boundary problem: you must know exactly how many proxy hops you own to pick the correct client IP from the comma list, and strip or overwrite untrusted inbound values at your edge. The standardized Forwarded header (RFC 7239) supersedes the de-facto X-Forwarded-* set but is less widely emitted. For L4 paths, the PROXY protocol (v1 text / v2 binary) carries the original tuple and must be enabled symmetrically. Misconfiguration here underlies IP-spoofing, allowlist bypass, log poisoning, and cache-poisoning via X-Forwarded-Host — treat these headers as attacker-controlled until proven proxy-originated.

10. Load balancing algorithms

Once the LB has a set of healthy backends, how does it choose which one gets the next request? That choice is the load balancing algorithm, and each one encodes an assumption about your traffic. Pick the algorithm whose assumption matches reality and load spreads evenly; pick the wrong one and you get a hot, overloaded backend next to idle ones.

10.1 Round Robin

Rotate through the targets in order: request 1 → #1, request 2 → #2, request 3 → #3, request 4 → #1, and so on. Dead simple and the default in many tools.

Fig 9.7 — Round robin cycles through the pool
   req1 ──► #1        req4 ──► #1        req7 ──► #1
   req2 ──► #2        req5 ──► #2        req8 ──► #2
   req3 ──► #3        req6 ──► #3        req9 ──► #3
   ──────────────────────────────────────────────────►  time

Assumes: every backend has equal capacity and every request costs roughly the same. Wins for homogeneous fleets with uniform, short requests (the classic stateless web tier). Fails when requests vary wildly in cost — one slow report-generation request can pile up on a backend while round robin keeps feeding it more, oblivious to how busy it already is.

10.2 Weighted Round Robin

Like round robin, but each backend gets a weight, and bigger weights receive proportionally more requests. A backend with weight 3 gets three requests for every one a weight-1 backend gets.

Assumes: you know each backend's relative capacity. Wins for heterogeneous fleets (some m5.large, some m5.2xlarge) and for canary deployments — give the new build weight 5 and the stable build weight 95 to send 5% of traffic to the canary. Fails when weights are stale or request cost is uneven, same as plain round robin.

10.3 Least Connections

Send the next request to the backend that currently has the fewest active connections. This adapts to reality instead of assuming it: a backend bogged down with slow requests accumulates open connections, so the LB naturally stops piling more onto it.

Fig 9.8 — Least connections sends work to the least-busy backend
   current active connections per backend:

   #1  ███████████   (11 active)
   #2  ███           ( 3 active)   ◄── next request goes HERE (fewest)
   #3  ██████        ( 6 active)

Assumes: active-connection count is a good proxy for how busy a backend is. Wins for long-lived or uneven requests — streaming, large uploads/downloads, slow database-backed queries, WebSocket connections. Fails if connection count does not reflect real load (e.g. many cheap idle keep-alive connections look "busy" but are not).

10.4 Weighted Least Connections, Least Response Time

Weighted Least Connections combines the two ideas: pick by fewest connections, but scaled by each backend's capacity weight, so a big box can hold more connections before it is considered "as busy" as a small one. Least Response Time (a.k.a. Least Time) goes further and routes to the backend with the best blend of low active connections and fastest recent response time — directly optimizing the metric users actually feel. It needs the LB to measure latency per backend, which L7 LBs can do because they see responses.

10.5 IP Hash / consistent hashing

Compute a hash of the client's source IP (or another key) and use it to pick a backend, so the same client always maps to the same backend. This is a poor-man's stickiness without cookies, and it is the basis of cache affinity: if backend #2 has cached this client's data, keep sending them to #2 so the cache stays warm.

🔑 Key point — why "consistent" hashing matters

Naive hashing (hash(ip) % N) has a nasty property: if N changes — one backend is added or removed — the modulus changes for almost every client, reshuffling nearly all of them to new backends and blowing away every warm cache at once. Consistent hashing fixes this by placing backends and clients on a hash ring, so removing one backend only re-maps the clients that were on that backend — a minimal reshuffle — while everyone else stays put. This "only the affected slice moves" property is why consistent hashing underpins distributed caches and sharded data stores, and it returns in detail in Part 13.

10.6 Random and Power-of-Two-Choices

Pure random picks a backend at random; with enough requests it spreads roughly evenly, but unlucky streaks can briefly overload one. The modern default at scale is Power-of-Two-Choices: pick two backends at random and send the request to whichever of the two has fewer active connections. This one tweak dramatically smooths the load — it gets almost all the benefit of full least-connections without the LB having to track and compare every backend on every request, which matters enormously when there are thousands of backends. Two random samples plus a comparison beats both pure random and naive round robin at scale, which is why it is the quiet default inside many modern proxies and service meshes (Part 13).

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

Match the algorithm's assumption to your request profile.

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

Algorithm choice interacts with fleet size, request-cost variance, and observability: global least-connections requires shared state that does not scale, which is why P2C (a randomized, near-stateless approximation) dominates large meshes. Weighting can be dynamic (driven by backend-reported load or EWMA latency). Consistent hashing trades even distribution for affinity and needs virtual nodes to avoid hot spots. The subtle failure is feedback-loop instability — latency-based routing can oscillate or herd — so production systems damp signals and add randomization (the same jitter logic that prevents retry storms, section 14 and Part 13).

11. Session persistence / stickiness

Sometimes you genuinely need the same client to keep hitting the same backend. The classic reason, straight from Part 7: an app that stores session state in the memory of one instance. If a user logs in and their session lives in instance #1's heap, then any later request the LB routes to #2 or #3 finds no session and the user appears logged out. Sticky sessions (session affinity) are the band-aid: the LB ensures a given client keeps returning to the instance that holds their session.

An L7 LB implements stickiness by injecting a cookie. AWS's ALB sets a cookie named AWSALB (or a custom app cookie you nominate); on each subsequent request the LB reads that cookie and routes the client back to the same target.

   later requests (cookie present):

                              ▼                 (others idle by comparison)
                         ┌─────────┐
                         │   #2    │  ◄── this client is now PINNED here
                         └─────────┘
⚠️ Why stickiness is an anti-pattern at scale

Stickiness fixes the symptom and creates three new problems. (1) It breaks even distribution — long-lived sticky clients pile onto a few backends, producing hot targets while freshly added instances sit nearly idle, undermining the whole point of load balancing. (2) It makes scaling and deploys painful — adding instances does not relieve existing sticky load, and draining a sticky instance strands its sessions. (3) Worst of all, it does not even give you availability: when a sticky target dies, every session pinned to it is lost — the exact users mid-checkout get logged out, which is what you were trying to prevent.

🔑 Key point — the real fix is to not need stickiness

The better answer is the one from Part 7: make the app stateless, or externalize session state into a shared store like Redis (Spring Session backed by Redis is the canonical pattern). Then any instance can serve any request because the session lives outside the instances, the LB is free to use any algorithm, scaling and deploys are trivial, and a dying instance loses nothing because it held nothing. Stickiness is acceptable as a stopgap for a legacy app you cannot refactor today; it is not a destination. If you reach for sticky sessions as your default design, you have skipped the Part 7 lesson that makes horizontal scaling work at all.

🟢 Beginner — say it simply

"Sticky sessions" means the load balancer sends the same visitor back to the same server every time, using a cookie. It is sometimes needed if a server remembers things about you in its own memory. But it is a band-aid: the load gets uneven, and if that one server dies you lose everything it remembered. The real fix is to store that memory somewhere shared (like Redis) so any server can handle you.

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

L7 stickiness uses an LB-generated cookie (e.g. AWSALB) or a designated app cookie to pin a client to a target. It compensates for in-memory session state but causes hot targets, complicates scaling and draining, and loses sessions on target death. Prefer statelessness or an external session store (Redis via Spring Session) so any instance can serve any request and the LB can balance freely.

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

Affinity trades load evenness and failure-resilience for cache/session locality; it is justified only when the locality benefit (warm in-memory cache, expensive-to-rebuild per-client state) genuinely outweighs the cost and you accept session loss on node failure. Where locality is the real goal, consistent hashing gives affinity that degrades gracefully on membership change, unlike cookie-pinning. The durable architecture moves state to an external store and treats instances as fungible — the precondition for autoscaling, spot instances, and aggressive rolling deploys.

12. The AWS ELB family — ALB and NLB

Time to make this concrete in the cloud you will actually deploy to. AWS's load-balancing service is ELB (Elastic Load Balancing) — that is the umbrella brand, not a single product. Under it sit several load balancer types; you will spend nearly all your time with two of them.

12.1 ALB — Application Load Balancer (L7)

The ALB (Application Load Balancer) is AWS's managed Layer 7 load balancer for HTTP, HTTPS, gRPC, and WebSocket. Its building blocks:

  • Listeners — a listener watches a port/protocol (e.g. HTTPS on 443) for incoming connections.
  • Rules — each listener has an ordered list of rules with conditions (path pattern, host header, HTTP header, query string, source IP) and actions (forward to a target group, redirect, return a fixed response). This is where path- and host-based routing lives.
  • Target groups — the backend pools. Targets can be EC2 instances, IP addresses (for ECS/EKS tasks, Part 12), or even Lambda functions. Each target group has its own health check (section 5).
  • TLS termination via ACM — the ALB terminates HTTPS using a certificate from ACM (AWS Certificate Manager, Part 8), which provisions and auto-renews the cert for free. The cert lives on the ALB; you never copy a private key onto a server.
  • WAF integration — attach AWS WAF to the ALB to filter malicious requests before they reach your app.

12.2 NLB — Network Load Balancer (L4)

The NLB (Network Load Balancer) is AWS's managed Layer 4 load balancer for TCP and UDP. It is built for ultra-high throughput and ultra-low latency — it can handle millions of requests per second (RPS) with single-digit-millisecond latency. Its distinguishing features:

  • Static / Elastic IP per Availability Zone — an NLB gives you a fixed IP in each AZ (and supports Elastic IPs), which is exactly what you need when a client or firewall must allowlist a specific IP. (An ALB only gives you a DNS name, with IPs that can change.)
  • Preserves the source IP — because it operates at L4 and can forward without SNAT, backends can see the real client IP directly (no X-Forwarded-For needed for the IP itself).
  • TLS passthrough or termination — an NLB can pass TLS straight through to the backend (end-to-end encryption, the LB never sees plaintext) or, optionally, terminate TLS itself.
  • Any TCP/UDP protocol — used to front non-HTTP services (databases, Kafka, custom protocols) and, very commonly, placed in front of ALBs to give an HTTP stack a static entry IP plus raw throughput.

Two more members, one line each: the CLB (Classic Load Balancer) is the original, now legacy and deprecated — do not use it for new work; the GWLB (Gateway Load Balancer) is a specialist for inserting inline third-party network appliances (firewalls, deep packet inspection) into your traffic path, which you will rarely touch as an app engineer.

🔑 Key point — both are HA by design

This is the payoff for section 4's worry. You never run an ALB or NLB as a single box. Both are multi-AZ, automatically scaled, managed services — AWS spreads the load balancer itself across Availability Zones (Part 11) and grows its capacity as your traffic grows. The load balancer is therefore not the single point of failure people fear; AWS has made it highly available on your behalf. When someone in an interview says "but the LB is a SPOF," the correct response is "a managed cloud LB is a horizontally-scaled, multi-AZ fleet, and an on-prem LB is run as an active/standby HA pair — it is the one component you specifically build not to be a single point of failure."

13.2 Weighted target groups — a 90/10 canary deploy

You want to ship a risky change to 10% of traffic first. A single ALB rule can forward to multiple target groups with weights:

# ALB listener rule: forward with weights (a canary release)
Action: forward
  target groups:
    - order-service-stable   weight: 90     # 90% of matching requests
    - order-service-canary   weight: 10     # 10% to the new build
# Watch error rates and latency on the canary target group.
# Looks good?  shift to 50/50, then 0/100.   Looks bad?  set canary weight back to 0 instantly.

This is weighted distribution (section 10) applied at the target-group level. If the canary's error rate or latency spikes, you set its weight to 0 and traffic instantly drains back to stable — a controlled, reversible rollout instead of a big-bang deploy.

Now a non-HTTP example where only an L4 LB will do. You run a three-node Redis (or Kafka, or PostgreSQL) cluster and want clients to use one stable endpoint instead of memorizing three changing instance IPs. An ALB cannot help — Redis is not HTTP. An NLB can, because it balances raw TCP:

14. Operational realities — 502 / 503 / 504 and timeouts

When you are on call and the load balancer is returning errors, the specific status code tells you precisely where the failure is. This is one of the highest-value debugging tables in backend engineering and a guaranteed interview topic; it builds directly on the HTTP status codes from Part 7.

StatusNameWhat the LB is telling youLikely cause
502Bad Gateway"I reached a backend, but it gave me garbage or closed the connection unexpectedly."Backend crashed mid-response, returned a malformed/non-HTTP reply, or dropped the connection. Often an app exception or a backend that died after the LB connected.

🔑 Key point — read the code, locate the fault

502 = backend answered badly (it is up but broken or just crashed). 503 = nobody is home (no healthy targets — check your health checks and deploys first). 504 = backend is too slow (timeout — check slow queries, thread pools, and the LB's timeout setting). Memorize this triple; it turns a vague "the LB is erroring" page into a precise first hypothesis, and being able to recite it cold is something interviewers specifically probe for.

14.1 Idle timeout kills long-lived connections

Every load balancer has an idle timeout — if no bytes flow on a connection for N seconds (ALB default 60), the LB closes it. This silently breaks two things backend engineers care about. First, long requests: a report endpoint that takes 90 seconds to respond gets its connection cut at 60 and the client sees a 504, even though the backend was working fine. Second, idle long-lived connections: a WebSocket or a long-held database connection that sits quiet longer than the idle timeout gets reaped. This connects straight back to Part 4's TCP keepalive — keepalives exist precisely to send periodic "still here" bytes so a connection is not mistaken for dead and torn down by an intermediary like a load balancer or NAT gateway. Tune the LB idle timeout above your longest legitimate request, and enable keepalives (or application-level heartbeats) for persistent connections.

14.2 Cross-zone load balancing, and the retry-storm danger

One-liner you should know: cross-zone load balancing lets a load balancer node in one Availability Zone send traffic to healthy targets in other AZs, smoothing imbalance when targets are unevenly spread across zones (on by default for ALB; opt-in, and historically with a data-transfer cost, for NLB).

⚠️ The thundering herd / retry storm

When a backend slows down and clients (or the LB) retry failed requests, those retries add more load to an already-struggling system, which makes it slower, which triggers more retries — a self-amplifying retry storm, a cousin of the "thundering herd" where many clients pile on at once. Naive retries turn a small blip into a full outage. The defenses — retry budgets (cap retries to a small fraction of total traffic so retries can never dominate), exponential backoff, and jitter (randomize retry timing so clients do not all retry in lockstep) — are core resilience patterns we develop properly in Part 13. For now, plant the instinct: retries without budgets and jitter make outages worse, not better.

🟢 Beginner — say it simply

When the load balancer shows an error, the number tells you what went wrong: 502 means a server answered but broke, 503 means there were no healthy servers at all, 504 means a server was too slow. Also: load balancers hang up on connections that go quiet too long, which can cut off slow requests or live connections like chat — that is why "keepalive" heartbeats exist.

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

502/503/504 localize faults to "backend replied badly / no healthy targets / backend timed out." Idle timeouts reap quiet connections (default ~60s), breaking long requests and idle WebSocket/DB connections unless you raise the timeout or use TCP keepalive (Part 4) / app heartbeats. Cross-zone balancing evens out uneven per-AZ target counts. Retries without budgets, backoff, and jitter cause retry storms that amplify outages.

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

Treat LB error codes as a layered diagnosis: 503 points at health-check/deploy/control-plane issues; 504 at backend latency, thread-pool saturation, or timeout misconfiguration (and timeouts must be coordinated end-to-end so an inner timeout fires before an outer one); 502 at protocol/connection-lifecycle mismatches (keep-alive races, backend closing pooled connections the LB still holds). Idle-timeout and keepalive settings must be reconciled across client, LB, and backend to avoid half-open connections. Retry safety requires budgets, capped exponential backoff with jitter, circuit breaking, and idempotency keys — without them, load balancers faithfully amplify a brownout into an outage.

16. Interview questions

🎤 Try answering out loud before you click
What is the difference between a Layer 4 and a Layer 7 load balancer, and when would you choose each?

An L4 load balancer operates at the transport layer and sees only the 5-tuple (source/destination IP and port, protocol). It forwards TCP/UDP flows without inspecting the payload — fast, protocol-agnostic (works for Postgres, Redis, Kafka, anything), and passes TLS through to the backend. An L7 load balancer terminates the connection, reads the full HTTP request (method, path, Host, headers, cookies), and can route by path/host, terminate TLS, inject sticky cookies, rewrite requests, rate-limit, and run a WAF — at the cost of HTTP parsing and being HTTP-only. Choose L7 for HTTP/HTTPS microservices needing content-based routing or TLS offload; choose L4 for non-HTTP traffic, ultra-high throughput/low latency, a static IP, or end-to-end TLS where the LB must not hold your certificate. On AWS these are the ALB and NLB respectively.

How do health checks work, and what is the deep-vs-shallow trade-off?

The LB actively probes each backend on an interval (e.g. GET /actuator/health every 15s), marking a target unhealthy after N consecutive failures and healthy again after N successes; it can also passively observe real-traffic errors. A shallow check tests only that the process is up and listening (TCP connect or a trivial 200). A deep check has the endpoint verify dependencies — database, Redis, downstream services. Deep checks catch "up but useless" instances, but they have a dangerous failure mode: if all instances share a database and the database blips, every deep check fails at once and the LB removes the whole fleet, turning a recoverable hiccup into a total outage. The fix is to scope health to per-instance health and separate liveness (am I up?) from readiness (can I serve?), rather than coupling a server's health to a shared dependency's health.

Explain round robin versus least connections. When does each win?

Round robin rotates through backends in order, assuming equal capacity and equal request cost — great for a homogeneous fleet handling short, uniform requests, but it keeps feeding a backend even when that backend is bogged down with slow requests. Least connections sends each request to the backend with the fewest active connections, adapting to real load — better for long-lived or uneven requests like streaming, large uploads, slow queries, or WebSockets, where some requests tie up a backend far longer than others. Least connections needs the LB to track active connections; round robin needs no state. Variants like weighted least connections add capacity awareness, and power-of-two-choices approximates least connections cheaply at very large scale.

What is X-Forwarded-For and why does it matter to a backend engineer?

When an L7 load balancer terminates the client connection and opens its own to the backend, the backend sees the LB's IP as the source, not the real client's. X-Forwarded-For is the header the LB injects to preserve the original client IP (with X-Forwarded-Proto and X-Forwarded-Host for the original scheme and host). It matters because without honoring it, your logs show the LB's IP, your IP-based rate limiting throttles the LB instead of clients, and — most painfully — request.getScheme() returns http behind a TLS-terminating LB, which can cause infinite HTTP→HTTPS redirect loops. In Spring Boot you set server.forward-headers-strategy=framework to fix it. Critically, you must only trust these headers from your own load balancer, since any client can forge them — trusting them blindly is an IP-spoofing/allowlist-bypass vulnerability. For L4 LBs the equivalent is the PROXY protocol.

What is the difference between an ALB and an NLB?

The ALB is AWS's managed Layer 7 load balancer for HTTP/HTTPS/gRPC/WebSocket: it does content-based routing via listeners and rules (path, host, header conditions) to target groups, terminates TLS using ACM certificates, and integrates with WAF — but it exposes only a DNS name and adds HTTP-parsing latency. The NLB is AWS's managed Layer 4 load balancer for TCP/UDP: ultra-high throughput (millions of RPS), single-digit-millisecond latency, a static/Elastic IP per AZ, source-IP preservation, and TLS passthrough or termination — but it cannot route by URL or read headers. Use the ALB for smart HTTP routing and TLS offload; use the NLB for non-HTTP services, when you need a static IP, for extreme scale, or fronting ALBs. Both are multi-AZ, auto-scaling, and inherently highly available.

How does a stateless application make load balancing easy?

If the app holds no per-client state in instance memory, then any instance can serve any request. The load balancer is free to send each request to any healthy backend using any algorithm, with no need for sticky sessions. Scaling is trivial (new instances immediately take load), deploys are trivial (drain and replace any instance with no session loss), and a crashing instance loses nothing because it held nothing. Statelessness (Part 7) is the precondition that makes horizontal scaling and the "cattle, not pets" model work; where state is unavoidable, you externalize it to a shared store like Redis (Spring Session) rather than pinning clients to instances.

What do 502, 503, and 504 from a load balancer tell you?

They localize the fault. 502 Bad Gateway means the LB reached a backend but got an invalid response or the backend closed the connection unexpectedly — the backend is up but broken or just crashed mid-response. 503 Service Unavailable means the LB has no healthy targets to route to — typically all targets failed health checks (a bad deploy, an empty target group, or a deep-health-check cascade). 504 Gateway Timeout means a backend accepted the request but did not respond within the LB's timeout — a slow query, a stuck thread, or a request that legitimately exceeds the configured idle/response timeout. So: 502 = backend answered badly, 503 = nobody healthy is home, 504 = backend too slow.

Is the load balancer itself a single point of failure?

It would be if you ran one — which is exactly why you never do. On-prem load balancers run as a redundant HA (high-availability) pair, typically active/standby sharing a virtual IP, so the standby takes over the VIP within seconds if the active fails. Managed cloud load balancers (AWS ALB/NLB) are horizontally-scaled, multi-AZ services that AWS spreads across Availability Zones and scales automatically — there is no single box to fail. So while the LB concentrates traffic, it is the one component specifically architected not to be a single point of failure. The correct framing is that the LB removes the backends' single-point-of-failure problem while being built redundantly so it does not reintroduce one.

Are load balancing and high availability the same thing?

No — they are related but distinct goals. Load balancing is about scale: spreading load across many servers to multiply capacity. High availability is about resilience: surviving the failure of any one server with no user-visible outage. A load balancer can deliver both, but via different features — capacity comes from the distribution algorithm, availability comes from health checks pulling dead targets out of rotation. You can have one without the other: a dumb splitter gives scale but not availability (it keeps sending to crashed servers), and an active/standby pair gives availability but little scale. A good load balancer combines them, but conflating the two is a classic mistake.

Why are sticky sessions considered an anti-pattern, and what is the better solution?

Sticky sessions pin each client to one backend (via an LB cookie like AWSALB) to support apps that keep session state in instance memory. They are an anti-pattern at scale because they break even distribution (long-lived clients create hot targets while new instances stay idle), complicate scaling and draining, and — fatally — lose every pinned session when that backend dies, which is the very thing you were trying to protect. The better solution is to make the app stateless or move session state to a shared store such as Redis (Spring Session). Then any instance serves any request, the LB balances freely, deploys and autoscaling are trivial, and an instance death loses nothing. Stickiness is acceptable only as a temporary stopgap for a legacy app, never as a default design.

Why does adding a load balancer not automatically make a stateful app scale?

A load balancer spreads requests, but if instances keep per-client state in their own memory, spreading those requests across instances breaks the app: request two may land on an instance that has never seen the user, so they appear logged out or lose their cart. You either bolt on sticky sessions (an anti-pattern that defeats even distribution and loses state on failure) or — the real fix — make the app stateless or externalize state to a shared store. The load balancer is necessary but not sufficient for horizontal scaling; statelessness (Part 7) is the other half. Adding an LB to a stateful app gives you the distribution mechanism without the property that makes distribution safe.

How does an L7 load balancer route requests to different services on one domain?

Because it terminates the connection and reads the HTTP request, an L7 LB can match on path, host, header, method, or query string and forward to different backend pools (target groups). On an AWS ALB you configure a listener (e.g. HTTPS:443) with ordered rules: /api/* → order-service target group, /auth/* → auth-service, default → frontend. Rules are evaluated by priority, first match wins, and a default action catches the rest. You can also weight a single rule across multiple target groups for canary releases (e.g. 90% stable, 10% canary). An L4 LB cannot do any of this because the path and headers live inside the payload it never inspects.

17. Common misconceptions

❌ Myths engineers actually believe

❌ "A load balancer is just round-robin."
✅ Round robin is one of several algorithms (least connections, weighted, IP/consistent hash, least response time, power-of-two-choices), and the algorithm is the least of what a load balancer does. The features that make it valuable are health checking (so it never routes to a dead server), connection draining (graceful deploys), L7 content routing, TLS termination, and high availability. A pure round-robin splitter with no health awareness is barely better than DNS round-robin.

❌ "An L4 load balancer can route by URL path."
✅ It cannot. An L4 LB sees only the 5-tuple — IP addresses and ports — and never opens the payload, so the URL path, Host header, and cookies are invisible to it. Path-based routing requires an L7 load balancer that terminates the connection and parses HTTP. If you need /api and /images to go to different pools, you need L7 (an ALB), not L4 (an NLB).

❌ "The load balancer is a single point of failure."
✅ Only if naively deployed as one box — which nobody does. On-prem LBs run as redundant active/standby HA pairs sharing a virtual IP; managed cloud LBs (ALB/NLB) are horizontally-scaled, multi-AZ services that AWS keeps highly available automatically. The load balancer is the one component you specifically build not to be a single point of failure.

❌ "Load balancing and high availability are the same thing."
✅ They are different goals served by different features. Load balancing delivers scale via the distribution algorithm; high availability delivers resilience via health checks that remove dead backends. You can have scale without availability (a splitter that feeds crashed servers) or availability without much scale (active/standby). A good LB provides both, but keep them conceptually separate.

❌ "Sticky sessions are a normal default."
✅ Stickiness is a band-aid for apps that wrongly keep session state in instance memory. It breaks even distribution (hot targets), complicates scaling and deploys, and loses every pinned session when a backend dies. The normal default is a stateless app or a shared session store (Redis), which lets any instance serve any request. Reaching for stickiness by default means you skipped the statelessness lesson that makes scaling work.

❌ "The backend automatically sees the real client IP."
✅ Behind an L7 LB that terminates the connection, the backend sees the load balancer's IP. The real client IP is carried in X-Forwarded-For (and the PROXY protocol for L4), and your app must be configured to read and trust it — otherwise logging, rate-limiting, and scheme detection all break, including the infamous redirect loop from getScheme() returning http. And you must trust the header only from your own LB, never from arbitrary clients.

❌ "Adding a load balancer automatically makes my stateful app scale."
✅ A load balancer distributes requests, but if instances hold per-client state in memory, distributing those requests across instances breaks the app — users land on instances that have never seen them. Horizontal scaling needs both a load balancer and statelessness (or a shared store). The LB is necessary but not sufficient; statelessness (Part 7) is the missing half.

❌ "A health check returning 200 means everything is fine."
✅ It only means whatever the health endpoint chose to test passed. A shallow check returning 200 says the process is listening, not that it can reach the database. A deep check returning 200 says dependencies are reachable but risks cascading the whole fleet down when a shared dependency blips. Designing what the health check verifies — and scoping it to per-instance health — is a real reliability decision, not a checkbox.
