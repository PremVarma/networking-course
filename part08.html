1. The problem: HTTP is a postcard

🧭 Why this part exists

Part 1 warned you that a WAN (Wide Area Network) is hostile territory: your data crosses infrastructure owned by strangers — your coffee shop, their ISP (Internet Service Provider), a backbone carrier, an IXP (Internet Exchange Point), Amazon's edge network. Part 7 showed you that an HTTP request is just readable text: GET /api/orders, a Cookie: header carrying your session, maybe a JSON body with a password in it. Put those two facts together: every byte of every HTTP message is readable, recordable, and editable by every network it crosses. This is problem #9 from Part 1's ten problems — the trust problem — and TLS (Transport Layer Security) is its answer.

Make it visceral. You open your laptop in a coffee shop and log in to a site over plain HTTP. Your request hops through the shop's Wi-Fi access point, the shop's router, their ISP, a metro exchange, a backbone carrier, and the server's hosting provider — easily 10–20 distinct devices owned by five or six organizations you have never heard of. Every single one of them sees this, verbatim:

This is not theoretical. In Part 2 you ran Wireshark to watch packets on your own machine. Run it on any shared network — open Wi-Fi, or any Wi-Fi where you know the password, which is every café and hotel — and plain-HTTP traffic from other people appears on your screen: URLs, headers, cookies, form fields, passwords. Radio is a broadcast medium (Part 1's physics section); "sending" on Wi-Fi means shouting into a room, and encryption is the only thing that makes shouting safe.

📜 The Firesheep moment (2010)

For years, big sites encrypted only their login page — the password was protected, but the session cookie (Part 7) that the login produced was then sent over plain HTTP with every subsequent request. In October 2010, developer Eric Butler released Firesheep, a Firefox extension that sat on a shared Wi-Fi network, sniffed those cookies out of the air, and showed a sidebar with the name and photo of every person browsing Facebook or Twitter nearby. Double-click a face and you were logged in as them. One click. No skill required. It was downloaded hundreds of thousands of times in days, and it did exactly what it was designed to do: shame the industry. Within months the big platforms began moving to HTTPS for every request, not just login — the start of the "HTTPS everywhere" era that, combined with free certificates (section 11), pushed the web from ~40% encrypted in 2015 to ~95%+ today.

Eavesdropping is only the polite attack. Networks in the middle can also change what passes through them — and real companies did this as a business model: several ISPs and hotel/airline Wi-Fi providers were caught injecting their own advertising banners and tracking scripts into plain-HTTP pages their customers were reading. The page that arrived was not the page the server sent:

Combine impersonation in both directions and you get the canonical network attack, the one every security mechanism in this part is measured against — MITM (Man-In-The-Middle). The attacker inserts themselves between you and the real server and relays traffic both ways, reading and editing at will. The devastating property: both ends see a perfectly normal conversation.

Hold these three attacks in your head — eavesdropping, tampering, impersonation — because TLS is not one defense; it is three defenses bundled, one per attack. That mapping is the next section.

2. The three security goals (and the one that is not)

Security engineers compress the requirements into three properties. Each one neutralizes exactly one of the attacks above, and each is implemented by a different cryptographic mechanism — this table is the skeleton of the entire part:

GoalPlain-English promiseDefeatsMechanismBuilt from (section)
ConfidentialityNobody in the middle can read the dataEavesdroppingEncryption with session keysSymmetric crypto (§4) + key exchange (§6)
IntegrityNobody in the middle can change the data undetectedTamperingMACs / AEAD — tamper-evidence on every recordHashing (§7)
AuthenticityYou are talking to the real server, not a look-alikeImpersonation / MITMCertificates + digital signaturesSignatures (§8) + certificates (§10–11)
🔑 Key point

All three goals are necessary, and authenticity is the one beginners forget. Encryption without authentication is worthless against a MITM: you would just establish a beautifully encrypted connection… directly to the attacker, who decrypts, reads, re-encrypts, and forwards. Most of TLS's apparent complexity — certificates, chains, authorities — exists to answer one question: whose key am I actually encrypting to?

Also note what is not on the list: availability. TLS does not keep your service up — an attacker who can drop your packets can still deny service, and a DDoS does not care about your certificates. Availability is the job of Parts 9–13 (load balancers, redundancy, autoscaling), not of cryptography.

3. Encryption from zero

You need exactly four words of vocabulary, and none of the math. (True to this course's style: we will build intuition for the crypto, and treat the algorithms as trustworthy black boxes — which is precisely how you should treat them in production code, too. Never write your own.)

  • Plaintext — the original, readable data. Your HTTP request.
  • Ciphertext — the scrambled, unreadable output that actually crosses the network.
  • Cipher — the algorithm that converts between the two. Public, standardized, heavily studied.
  • Key — the secret input that makes your scrambling unique. A key is just a number — typically 128 or 256 random bits.

One principle governs the whole field, formulated by the cryptographer Auguste Kerckhoffs in 1883: a system must remain secure even if everything about it except the key is public knowledge. Security lives in the key, never in the secrecy of the algorithm. This is why AES's full specification is a public document anyone can read, and why "we use a proprietary secret algorithm" is the reddest of red flags — secret algorithms get no peer review, and history shows they fall fast. The entire design question of TLS therefore reduces to: how do two machines get a shared secret key, and which public algorithms do they run with it?

4. Symmetric encryption — one key, blazing fast, one fatal flaw

Symmetric encryption uses a single shared key for both directions: the same key that encrypts also decrypts. Both parties must hold an identical copy.

          │                                                │

The reigning symmetric cipher is AES (Advanced Encryption Standard) — standardized by the US NIST in 2001 after a public worldwide competition (Kerckhoffs would approve). You will meet it as AES-128 or AES-256 (the key size in bits; both are beyond brute force — 2128 is a number with 38 digits) combined with a mode of operation such as GCM (Galois/Counter Mode), which we will demystify in section 7. Read AES_128_GCM as "AES with a 128-bit key, run in GCM mode" — that string appears verbatim inside TLS cipher suites later.

Symmetric crypto's superpower is speed. Modern CPUs have dedicated silicon for it — AES-NI (AES New Instructions), hardware instructions that encrypt multiple gigabytes per second per core. Encrypting your API traffic costs roughly 1–2% CPU; for bulk data movement it is effectively free. This matters for a misconception we will kill later: "TLS is too slow" stopped being true around 2010.

🔑 Key point — the fatal flaw: key distribution

Symmetric encryption has a chicken-and-egg problem that motivates everything else in this part: both sides need the same secret key before they can talk securely. But your browser and amazon.com have never met. The only channel between them is the network itself — the very wire the attacker is reading. Send the key over that wire in plaintext and the eavesdropper grabs it and decrypts everything after. Encrypt the key… with what key? You cannot bootstrap a shared secret over a public channel using symmetric tools alone. For most of human history this was solved by couriers with briefcases. The web needed something better, and the next two sections are the two breakthroughs (both from the 1970s) that provide it.

5. Asymmetric encryption — the padlock and the key

The first breakthrough: what if encrypting and decrypting used different keys? In asymmetric (public-key) cryptography, every participant generates a mathematically linked key pair:

  • The public key — shared with the entire world, printed on billboards if you like. It can encrypt messages (and, later, verify signatures).
  • The private key — never leaves your machine, ever. It is the only thing that can decrypt what the public key encrypted (and the only thing that can sign).
🌍 Analogy — the padlock-and-key mailbox

Imagine you hand out thousands of identical open padlocks, free, to anyone who asks — but you keep the one key that opens them locked in your desk. Anyone in the world can put a message in a box, snap one of your padlocks shut on it, and mail it to you. Snapping a padlock shut requires no secret — that is the public key. But once snapped, not even the sender can reopen the box. Only you can, with the one key you never gave anyone — the private key. Key distribution is solved: the thing you distribute (open padlocks) is useless for reading mail; the thing that reads mail (your key) is never distributed. An eavesdropper who intercepts a padlock can send you secure mail — but cannot open anyone else's.

   ANYONE ON EARTH                                    SERVER (key owner)

Two algorithm families carry the world's asymmetric load — know their names and one-line characters:

  • RSA (Rivest–Shamir–Adleman, 1977) — the venerable original; security rests on the difficulty of factoring enormous numbers. Big keys (2048–4096 bits), slow, utterly battle-tested. Still the most common key type inside certificates.
  • Elliptic-curve cryptography — the modern family: same security from far smaller, faster keys (256 bits). You will meet it as ECDHE (Elliptic Curve Diffie–Hellman Ephemeral) for key agreement (next section), ECDSA (Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm) and Ed25519 for signatures (Ed25519 is also what your modern SSH keys use).

So why not simply encrypt the whole HTTP conversation with the server's public key and be done? Because of the catch: asymmetric operations are 100–1000× slower than symmetric ones — heavyweight math on huge numbers, with no AES-NI-style hardware shortcut, plus message-size limits and other practical constraints. Public-key crypto is a scalpel, not a conveyor belt.

🔑 Key point — real systems are hybrid

Every serious protocol — TLS, SSH, Signal — uses the same two-phase pattern: asymmetric cryptography briefly, at the start, to agree on a small shared secret; then symmetric cryptography (AES) for the actual conversation. The expensive scalpel solves key distribution once per connection; the cheap conveyor belt encrypts the gigabytes. When you see the TLS handshake in section 13, it is exactly this: a short asymmetric ceremony whose only output is a symmetric session key.

🟢 Beginner — say it simply

Symmetric encryption: one shared key, like a house key both of you copy — fast, but how do strangers share it safely? Asymmetric encryption: a public padlock anyone can snap shut and a private key only you hold — solves the sharing problem, but it is slow. Real systems use the padlock once to exchange a house key, then use the house key for everything.

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

Symmetric (AES-128/256, usually in GCM mode) gives multi-GB/s throughput via AES-NI but requires pre-shared keys. Asymmetric (RSA, elliptic-curve) splits encrypt/verify (public) from decrypt/sign (private), enabling secure first contact at 100–1000× the CPU cost. TLS is hybrid: public-key operations bootstrap a per-connection symmetric session key. Kerckhoffs's principle throughout: algorithms public, keys secret.

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

In modern TLS the server's asymmetric key pair is used for authentication only (signing the handshake), not for encrypting application data and — since TLS 1.3 — not even for encrypting the key exchange. Session keys come from ephemeral Diffie–Hellman (next section), precisely so that compromise of the long-term private key does not decrypt recorded traffic. Also on the horizon: elliptic-curve and RSA math both fall to large quantum computers, which is why TLS deployments are already rolling out hybrid post-quantum key exchange (e.g. X25519 combined with ML-KEM) — the symmetric layer (AES) is essentially fine as-is.

6. Diffie–Hellman — agreeing on a secret in public

The second 1970s breakthrough is stranger and, for modern TLS, more important. Diffie–Hellman key agreement (Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman, 1976) lets two strangers, shouting across a room full of eavesdroppers, compute a shared secret that the eavesdroppers cannot derive — without ever transmitting the secret at all. No padlocks shipped, nothing secret crosses the wire. It sounds impossible. The classic paint-mixing analogy shows why it is not:

🌍 Analogy — mixing paint in public

Mixing paint is a one-way operation: trivial to do, practically impossible to undo — you cannot look at a bucket of olive-green and recover the exact two colors that made it. Diffie–Hellman is built on a mathematical operation with the same property (easy forward, infeasible backward). Alice and Bob agree — publicly — on a common starting color. Each then mixes in a private color they tell no one. They swap the resulting mixtures in full view of the eavesdropper, and each mixes their own private color into the mixture they received. Both now hold the same three-part blend: common + Alice's + Bob's. The eavesdropper holds the common color and both intermediate mixtures — but without the ability to un-mix, cannot produce the final blend.

        ALICE                     public wire                      BOB
          │                            │                            │
          │      common paint: YELLOW (public, everyone sees it)    │
          │◄───────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────►│
          │                            │                            │
   + secret RED                        │                     + secret BLUE
   = ORANGE mix                        │                     = GREEN mix
          │                            │                            │

In the real protocol the "colors" are numbers, the "mixing" is modular exponentiation (classic DH) or elliptic-curve point math (ECDH — Elliptic Curve Diffie–Hellman, the version everything uses today), and the final shared color becomes the input from which the AES session keys are derived. The eavesdropper sees the public parameters and both public "mixtures" (called key shares) and can do nothing with them.

6.1 Ephemeral keys and forward secrecy

Now the detail with enormous real-world consequences. The "E" in ECDHE stands for ephemeral: both sides generate a brand-new DH key pair for every connection and throw it away when the connection closes. The secret colors live in RAM for the duration of one session, then cease to exist. This buys a property called forward secrecy:

🔑 Key point — forward secrecy

Forward secrecy means yesterday's recorded traffic stays unreadable even if the server's long-term private key leaks tomorrow. Threat model: a well-resourced adversary records your ciphertext for years (storage is cheap), then later steals or compels the server's private key. In old-style TLS — where the client encrypted the session key directly with the certificate's RSA public key — that stolen key retroactively decrypts every recorded session ever. With ephemeral Diffie–Hellman, the certificate's key never encrypts anything; each session's secret came from throwaway key shares that no longer exist. There is nothing to steal. This is why TLS 1.3 made ephemeral key exchange mandatory and deleted the old RSA key-transport mode entirely — and it should also reshape your intuition: the certificate's job is identity, not encryption. Hold that thought for section 10.

🟢 Beginner — say it simply

Diffie–Hellman lets two strangers create a shared secret by exchanging "mixtures" that are useless to eavesdroppers — like mixing paint: easy to combine, impossible to separate. Doing it with fresh throwaway values every session ("ephemeral") means even a future theft of the server's keys cannot unlock past conversations.

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

Each side picks a random private value, derives a public key share, and combines its private value with the peer's share; the math guarantees both arrive at the same shared secret while observers cannot. TLS runs this as ECDHE over curves like X25519, feeds the result through a key-derivation function (HKDF), and produces separate AES keys per direction. Ephemeral = per-connection key pairs = forward secrecy; mandatory in TLS 1.3.

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

DH alone is unauthenticated — an active MITM can run two separate DH exchanges, one with each victim (the paint analogy has no defense against someone who intercepts and re-mixes). That is why TLS pairs ECDHE with a certificate signature over the handshake transcript: the key exchange provides secrecy, the signature proves which endpoint you exchanged with. Forward secrecy also changed operations: TLS-inspecting middleboxes that relied on holding the server's RSA key to passively decrypt traffic stopped working with TLS 1.3, forcing enterprises to explicit MITM proxies with their own internal CA (Certificate Authority) — you will recognize this as your corporate laptop's "weird root certificate."

7. Hashing — the fingerprint function

Third tool. A cryptographic hash function takes any input — one byte or a 4 GB file — and produces a fixed-size digest (for SHA-256 — Secure Hash Algorithm, 256-bit variant — always exactly 32 bytes). Three properties make it a fingerprint:

  • Fixed size, any input — the digest length never changes.
  • One-way — given a digest, finding an input that produces it is computationally infeasible. It is a meat grinder: sausage from pig, never pig from sausage.
  • Avalanche effect — flip a single bit of input and roughly half the output bits flip; digests of near-identical inputs look totally unrelated.

🌍 Analogy — the wax seal

A medieval letter was folded and sealed with wax pressed with the sender's ring. The seal does not hide the contents (that is encryption's job) — it makes tampering evident: any attempt to open and alter the letter breaks the seal, and only the ring's owner can re-impress it. A MAC (Message Authentication Code) is a wax seal for bytes: a hash computed over the message plus the shared secret key, stamped onto every TLS record. The receiver recomputes it; if even one bit was altered in transit, the avalanche effect makes the seal mismatch screamingly obvious, and the connection is killed. An attacker cannot forge a fresh seal without the ring — the key.

One honest paragraph on the acronyms you will see. HMAC (Hash-based Message Authentication Code) is the classic keyed-hash construction (it is also what you use to verify webhook signatures from Stripe or GitHub — same wax seal, different letter). Older TLS encrypted the data and separately attached an HMAC. Modern TLS instead uses AEAD (Authenticated Encryption with Associated Data) ciphers — this is what the GCM in AES_128_GCM means: one integrated mode that encrypts AND authenticates in a single pass, producing ciphertext plus a built-in authentication tag. Confidentiality and integrity, one operation, fewer ways to combine the pieces wrongly (several historical TLS attacks exploited exactly those bad combinations). TLS 1.3 permits only AEAD ciphers.

8. Digital signatures — the atom certificates are built from

Now run asymmetric crypto backwards and something wonderful falls out. Encryption used the public key to lock and the private key to unlock. Flip it: what if the private key transforms a message, and the public key checks the transformation? Then anyone holding your public key can verify that the transformation was made by your private key — i.e., by you — because nobody else could have produced it.

That is a digital signature. The intuition-level recipe (real schemes like RSA-PSS, ECDSA and Ed25519 differ in details, but this is the right mental model):

Fig 8.9 — Signing and verifying
   SIGNER (holds private key)                 VERIFIER (holds public key)
        │                                            │
   document ──► SHA-256 ──► digest                   │  receives: document + signature
        │                     │                      │
        │      "encrypt" digest with                 │  1. SHA-256(document) ──► digest A
        │       PRIVATE key = SIGNATURE              │  2. check signature with
        │                     │                      │     PUBLIC key ──► digest B
        └──── send document + signature ───────────► │  3. digest A == digest B ?
                                                     │
                                              match    ──► signed by the private-key
                                                           holder, unmodified since
                                              mismatch ──► forged OR tampered. reject.

Why hash first? Speed and size: asymmetric math on a 32-byte digest instead of a 2 MB document. The avalanche effect guarantees the digest is the document for integrity purposes. A valid signature proves two things at once:

  • Authenticity — the private-key holder, and only them, produced it.
  • Integrity — the document has not changed by a single bit since signing (else the digests diverge).

And a bonus third: non-repudiation — the signer cannot later deny signing, because nobody else could have. (A MAC cannot give you this: both ends share the MAC key, so either could have produced any given seal. Signatures are asymmetric; the power to sign is not shared.)

🔑 Key point

A digital signature is a verifiable claim bound to a key pair: "the holder of private key X vouches for exactly these bytes." Everything in the next three sections — certificates, certificate chains, Certificate Authorities, the trust decisions your browser makes 100 times a day — is just this atom, composed: documents that say "this public key belongs to this domain name," signed by parties you already trust. Understand Fig 8.9 and you understand PKI (Public Key Infrastructure).

9. SSL vs TLS — one protocol, two names, and a layer question

📜 What came before — from SSL to TLS

The web's first commercial boom (1994–95) needed credit-card numbers to cross the wire, so Netscape built SSL (Secure Sockets Layer). SSL 1.0 was so flawed it never shipped; SSL 2.0 (1995) and SSL 3.0 (1996) carried the early web but are now thoroughly broken — the 2014 POODLE attack (Padding Oracle On Downgraded Legacy Encryption) was SSL 3.0's final nail, and both versions are formally prohibited today. Standardization passed to the IETF (Internet Engineering Task Force), which — partly to neutralize vendor branding — renamed the protocol TLS (Transport Layer Security): TLS 1.0 (1999, a light revision of SSL 3.0), TLS 1.1 (2006), TLS 1.2 (2008 — the long-reigning workhorse, still everywhere), and TLS 1.3 (2018, RFC 8446 — a genuine redesign: leaner, faster, with every known-weak option amputated). So: SSL is the dead ancestor, TLS is the living protocol — yet the industry still says "SSL certificate," "SSL termination," and names libraries OpenSSL. Treat every modern utterance of "SSL" as a fossil phrase meaning TLS. An "SSL certificate" is a TLS certificate; there is no difference, and the certificate is version-agnostic anyway.

Which OSI layer is that, exactly? Here is the honest answer, continuing Part 2's reality check about Layers 5 and 6: the OSI layers blur here, and that is fine. TLS establishes and manages a session (very Layer 5), transforms data representation by encrypting it (very Layer 6), yet rides directly on TCP (Layer 4) and is configured by application-layer software (Layer 7). Textbooks file it under "5/6"; the IETF — which never adopted OSI's seven layers anyway — just calls it "above the transport, below the application," and its own name says Transport Layer Security. The pragmatic model to carry: TLS is a transparent secure pipe inserted between TCP and HTTP. HTTP does not know it is being encrypted; TCP does not know what it is carrying; both behave exactly as Parts 4 and 7 taught you. (This layering independence is precisely why the same TLS protects SMTP, IMAP, PostgreSQL connections, and gRPC without modification — and why Part 9 can casually "terminate" it at a load balancer.)

10. Certificates — the chain of trust

Sections 4–8 built the toolbox: fast symmetric encryption, key agreement that defeats eavesdroppers, seals that defeat tampering, signatures that prove identity. One gap remains, and it is the MITM-shaped gap from section 2: when your browser receives "the server's public key," how does it know the key belongs to amazon.com and not to the attacker relaying in the middle? A key is just a number; numbers carry no name tags. The answer is a document that binds a public key to a name, vouched for by someone you already trust: a certificate.

🌍 Analogy — sealed envelope, signature, and a notarized ID

A stranger hands you a sealed envelope (encryption) with a signature on the flap (integrity, authenticity of the sender's key). But a signature only proves "the same entity who holds this pen signed this" — it does not prove who that entity is. For that, humans invented the notarized ID: a document that says "this photo, this name, this person," issued and stamped by an authority everyone already recognizes — a passport office. You trust the stranger's claimed name not because of anything they say, but because a third party you already trust examined them and put its un-forgeable stamp on the binding. An X.509 certificate is exactly a notarized ID for a public key: "this public key ↔ this domain name," stamped — that is, digitally signed (section 8) — by a Certificate Authority your browser already trusts. The envelope, the signature, and the notarized ID together give you all three goals from section 2.

Certificates use a format called X.509. Run openssl x509 -in cert.pem -text against any site's certificate and you get something like this — these are the fields that matter, annotated:

  • SAN (Subject Alternative Name) is the field that matters. Historically the hostname lived in the Subject's CN (Common Name); today browsers ignore CN entirely and match the URL's hostname against the SAN list only. A cert is valid for exactly the names enumerated there. "Certificate is valid but browser rejects it" is very often "the hostname you used is not in the SANs."
  • The signature covers everything. Change one bit — extend the expiry, add a SAN — and the CA's signature no longer verifies (avalanche effect, section 7). A certificate is tamper-proof, which is why it can safely travel over the untrusted network during the handshake.

10.1 But who vouches for the voucher? The chain

Verifying example.com's certificate requires the issuer's public key. Where does that come from? Another certificate — which is signed by yet another issuer. The recursion must stop somewhere, and it stops at a root certificate: self-signed (it vouches for itself), and trusted not because of any signature but because it is pre-installed in your operating system's or browser's trust store — a curated list of roughly 150 root CAs that ships with Windows, macOS, Android, iOS, Firefox, and the JVM (Java Virtual Machine — its copy lives in a file called cacerts; remember that name for section 17).

Why the middle layer? Roots stay offline. A root CA's private key is the crown jewel — if it leaks, every certificate under it becomes forgeable and removing the root breaks half the web. So root keys live in HSMs (Hardware Security Modules) in physical vaults, powered off, and are used only to sign a few intermediate certificates. The intermediates do the millions of daily leaf signings; if one is compromised, it alone is revoked and replaced. Your server is expected to send the leaf plus the intermediates during the handshake (the root is never sent — the client already has it). The classic outage: "works in Chrome, fails from Java/curl" because the server sent only the leaf and Chrome papered over it by fetching the missing intermediate while stricter clients refused — always configure the full chain (fullchain.pem, not cert.pem).

11. Certificate Authorities — the trust business

🧭 Why trust anchors must exist

Could we do without CAs (Certificate Authorities)? Consider the alternatives. Option A: exchange keys in person. Perfect security, zero scale — you cannot fly to Seattle to swap USB sticks with Amazon before shopping. Option B: trust-on-first-use (TOFU) — accept whatever key the server shows the first time and alarm if it ever changes. This is exactly what SSH does (that "fingerprint… (yes/no)?" prompt on first connect, and the scary @@@ WARNING when a host key changes). TOFU is honest about its failure mode: it is blind on first contact — if the MITM is present at first connect, you trusted the attacker, and it cannot distinguish "server legitimately re-keyed" from "attack in progress." Tolerable for engineers connecting to their own servers; useless for two billion browsers visiting millions of new sites daily. Option C is the web's answer: pre-install a small set of professional verifiers — trust anchors — and have them vouch for everyone else. Centralized trust is the engineering compromise that scales; the rest of this section is how it works and how it fails.

11.1 How issuance actually works

You cannot buy trust for a domain you do not control — the CA's whole job is verifying control. The flow, which you will run yourself someday:

  1. Generate a key pair on your server. The private key never leaves it — not even the CA sees it.
  2. Create a CSR (Certificate Signing Request): a small signed file containing your public key and the names you want (api.example.com…). Send it to the CA.
  3. The CA challenges you to prove domain control, one of two classic ways:
    • HTTP challenge — "place this random token at http://api.example.com/.well-known/acme-challenge/…." Only someone controlling the server answering for that name can.
    • DNS challenge — "create a TXT record _acme-challenge.api.example.com with this random value." Part 6 promised those odd TXT records would matter; this is where. (DNS challenge is also the only way to get wildcard certs.)
  4. The CA signs your certificate with its intermediate key and hands it back. You install cert + chain next to your private key. Done.

Until 2015 this cost real money and manual effort, so half the web stayed on HTTP. Then Let's Encrypt — a nonprofit CA — made certificates free and defined ACME (Automatic Certificate Management Environment), a protocol that automates the entire flow above. A client like certbot (or Caddy, or Traefik, or your Kubernetes cert-manager) requests, proves, installs, and — because Let's Encrypt certs deliberately live only 90 days — renews on a timer, forever, with no human involved. Today Let's Encrypt issues for hundreds of millions of domains; ACME is the single biggest reason the web went from mostly-HTTP to mostly-HTTPS.

Vocabulary you will meet when buying or auditing certs:

  • DV (Domain Validation) — proves domain control only (the challenges above). What Let's Encrypt issues; what almost everyone needs.
  • OV / EV (Organization / Extended Validation) — the CA additionally verifies the legal entity behind the domain. EV once turned the address bar green with the company name; browsers removed that UI (users ignored it), so EV is now mostly a compliance checkbox.
  • Wildcard certificate — SAN of *.example.com matches api.example.com and www.example.com but — one level only — not a.b.example.com and not the bare example.com (so real wildcard certs carry both *.example.com and example.com as SANs).
  • CAA (Certification Authority Authorization) — the DNS record from Part 6's tease: example.com. CAA 0 issue "letsencrypt.org" tells every other CA on Earth "refuse to issue for this domain." CAs are required to check it. Cheap, set-and-forget protection against mis-issuance.

11.2 When the trust business fails

📜 DigiNotar, 2011 — what CA compromise looks like

DigiNotar was a small Dutch CA whose systems were breached in 2011; the intruder issued themselves more than 500 fraudulent certificates, including one for *.google.com that was then used to MITM the Gmail traffic of an estimated 300,000 users in Iran. Browser vendors responded with the death penalty: every DigiNotar root was ripped out of every trust store within days, instantly invalidating all its certificates — and the company was bankrupt within the month. The lesson is structural, not historical: any one of the ~150 trusted roots can technically sign for any domain on Earth, so the system is only as strong as its sloppiest member.

Two mechanisms now contain that structural risk. Certificate Transparency (CT): since 2018, browsers require every certificate to be published in public, append-only, cryptographically verifiable logs before they will accept it. Anyone can watch the logs — and companies do, with automated monitors that alarm the moment any CA anywhere issues a certificate for their domains. Mis-issuance can no longer be quiet, which changes the game from "prevent" (impossible) to "detect within minutes" (routine). Try it yourself: crt.sh lets you search the CT logs for any domain — including yours.

And revocation — honestly, the weakest part of the system. In theory a compromised cert is revoked via CRLs (Certificate Revocation Lists) — periodically downloaded blocklists — or OCSP (Online Certificate Status Protocol), a live "is this cert still good?" query to the CA. In practice both leak: CRLs are stale by design, OCSP adds latency and a privacy hole (the CA learns what you visit), and because CAs go down, browsers treat OCSP failure as a soft pass — an attacker who can MITM you can usually also block the revocation check. The industry's pragmatic conclusion: make certificates so short-lived that revocation barely matters — 90 days today, with maximum validity already legislated down toward ~47 days by 2029. Short-lived + automated beats long-lived + revocable.

🧪 Real example — certificate expiry, a top-5 outage class

On 3 February 2020, Microsoft Teams went down worldwide for hours because one TLS certificate expired — every client suddenly refused to talk to a server presenting an out-of-validity cert. Spotify, LinkedIn, Cisco gear, GitHub integrations, national COVID apps — the postmortem archives are full of identical incidents, and "the cert expired" reliably makes every list of most common self-inflicted outages. The fix is operational, not cryptographic: automate renewal (ACME everywhere, or AWS ACM — Certificate Manager — which auto-renews, section 17) and monitor expiry independently (alert at 30/14/7 days — your monitoring stack and even openssl s_client in a cron job can do it). If a human has to remember your renewals, you have already scheduled an outage.

🟢 Beginner — say it simply

A certificate is an ID card for a website: "this public key belongs to this domain," stamped by a Certificate Authority your device already trusts. Authorities prove you own a domain by making you place a secret file or DNS record, sign your certificate for ~90 days, and tools renew it automatically. Browsers check the stamp chain up to a built-in list of trusted roots.

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

X.509 leaf certs bind SANs to a public key, signed by an intermediate whose cert is signed by an offline root pre-installed in trust stores. Issuance: key pair + CSR + domain-control challenge (HTTP file or DNS TXT) via ACME. CT logs make all issuance public; CAA restricts which CAs may issue; revocation (CRL/OCSP) is best-effort, so validity keeps shrinking. Servers must serve leaf + intermediates, in order.

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

Treat PKI (Public Key Infrastructure) as a distributed system with failure modes: trust-store divergence (JVM cacerts vs OS store vs Docker base image — "works in browser, fails in Java" is usually a missing intermediate or stale cacerts), cross-signing and root rotation (Let's Encrypt's old root expiring in 2021 broke millions of legacy Android devices and embedded clients), and clock skew (cert validation needs correct time — a server with a drifted clock rejects valid certs, one reason NTP matters). Mature orgs run CT monitoring, CAA, ACME with two CAs configured for failover, and expiry alerting as standard hygiene.

12. What the browser actually validates

Every HTTPS connection, your browser runs this checklist in milliseconds. Knowing it cold is the difference between guessing and diagnosing when a TLS error appears:

🔑 The padlock checklist — all must pass
  • ✅ Chain verifies — every signature in leaf → intermediate → root checks out, and the root is in my trust store.
  • ✅ Validity window — today is between notBefore and notAfter for every cert in the chain.
  • ✅ Hostname matches — the hostname I typed appears in the leaf's SAN list (wildcards: one level).
  • ✅ Not revoked — best-effort OCSP/CRL check (soft-fail, as admitted above).
  • ✅ Acceptable cryptography — modern signature/hash algorithms (SHA-1 certs are dead), adequate key sizes, a protocol version ≥ TLS 1.2, plus a valid CT proof.
  • …and only then does the handshake proceed and the padlock render.
🔑 Key point — what the padlock does NOT mean

The padlock asserts exactly one sentence: "your connection to the domain in the address bar is encrypted, tamper-evident, and really terminates at a server that proved control of that domain." It says nothing about whether that domain is honest. arnazon-secure-login.com can hold a perfectly valid, free, padlock-producing certificate — phishing sites overwhelmingly use HTTPS now, because certificates are free and users were trained to look for the lock. The padlock authenticates the connection, not the business. Verify the domain name; the padlock cannot do it for you.

13. The TLS 1.2 handshake — the classic two-round-trip dance

Time to assemble every piece into the protocol itself. TLS 1.2 (2008) is no longer the latest, but learn it first: it is still everywhere in enterprise, and 1.3 only makes sense as a critique of it. The handshake is a negotiation — client and server agree on algorithms, authenticate, and derive keys — and like Part 4's TCP handshake, it is best read as a ladder:

Three annotations worth tattooing on:

  • Authentication happens at step 4, not step 3. Anyone can replay amazon.com's certificate — it is public. What an impersonator cannot do is produce the signature over the fresh ECDHE parameters and both randoms, because that requires the certificate's private key. The signed ServerKeyExchange is the moment "this peer holds the private key matching the certed public key" is proven, and the randoms make it un-replayable.
  • The Finished messages each carry a MAC over the entire handshake transcript, computed with the new session keys — proof that both sides derived the same keys and that no MITM downgraded or edited any earlier handshake message. Tamper-evidence applied to the negotiation itself.
  • Count the round trips: two, before a single HTTP byte. Recall Part 4's RTT (round-trip time) arithmetic: at 70 ms RTT (roughly Mumbai ↔ Singapore), TCP costs 70 ms, TLS 1.2 costs 140 ms — 210 ms of pure ceremony before the request even departs, then 70 ms more for the response. Protocol designers stare at that number with hatred; section 14 is what the hatred produced.

13.1 Decoding a cipher suite, character by character

The "cipher suite" the handshake negotiates is a fixed menu of the four tools you now know. The strings look like keyboard-mashing until you read them in segments:

   TLS _ ECDHE _ RSA _ WITH _ AES_128_GCM _ SHA256
         ──┬──   ─┬─          ─────┬─────   ──┬───

           │      │                │          └─ HASH for key derivation

A suite starting TLS_RSA_WITH_… (no ECDHE — the client encrypts the session key with the cert's RSA key) is the forward-secrecy-free dinosaur that 1.3 exterminated.

14. The TLS 1.3 handshake — one round trip, almost everything encrypted

TLS 1.3 (2018, RFC 8446) is a ruthless redesign with two obsessions: remove round trips and remove foot-guns. The trick for the first: since every modern connection was going to use ECDHE anyway, the client stops asking permission — it sends its DH key share optimistically, inside the very first message:

And notice the box: because both sides can compute the shared secret immediately after the key shares cross, everything after ServerHello is already encrypted — including the certificate itself. About that 0-RTT teaser: a client that has connected before can send "early data" — an HTTP request encrypted under a key from the previous session — with its very first packet, zero round trips of ceremony. The catch, in one sentence: 0-RTT early data can be replayed by an attacker who recorded it (the server cannot tell a replay from the original), so it is only safe for idempotent requests (Part 7's vocabulary — GETs, never payments) and many deployments simply leave it off.

14.1 Session resumption — why it matters at scale

Even 1-RTT handshakes carry real cost at scale: the asymmetric math (the certificate signature, the DH computation) is the most CPU-expensive thing a TLS server does. So TLS lets a server hand the client an encrypted session ticket — a resumable snapshot of the established keys. On the next connection the client presents the ticket and both sides skip certificate exchange and verification entirely (1.3 still folds in a fresh DH for forward secrecy). For a browser loading a site, or a mobile app reconnecting on every network blip, or a service mesh making millions of short connections, resumption plus connection reuse (Part 7's keep-alive) is the difference between TLS being free and TLS being your CPU bill. CDNs (Content Delivery Networks) and load balancers (Part 9) lean on it heavily.

🟢 Beginner — say it simply

The handshake is: hello + "here's what I speak" → "here's my choice + my ID card + proof I own it" → both compute the same secret key → everything after is encrypted. TLS 1.2 needs two round trips of this; TLS 1.3 compresses it to one by sending key material optimistically, and repeat visits can skip most of it with a saved ticket.

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

1.2: ClientHello/ServerHello negotiate a suite; the cert authenticates via the signature on ServerKeyExchange's ECDHE params; ClientKeyExchange completes DH; Finished MACs the transcript. 2 RTT. 1.3: key_share in ClientHello enables encryption from ServerHello onward; CertificateVerify signs the transcript; 1 RTT; static RSA, weak ciphers, and plaintext certs are gone; resumption via tickets, 0-RTT with replay caveats.

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

The transcript signature (CertificateVerify) is what binds the DH exchange to the certified identity — without it, ECDHE is MITM-able (§6 advanced). Downgrade protection: a 1.3 server forced to answer 1.2 embeds a sentinel in its random that 1.3 clients detect. Performance realities: handshake CPU is dominated by the server's signature (ECDSA P-256 ≫ RSA-2048 for signing speed — one reason for EC certs), session tickets shift state to clients but rotate ticket keys or you quietly weaken forward secrecy, and QUIC (HTTP/3, teased in Part 7) embeds this exact TLS 1.3 handshake inside the transport to merge TCP+TLS setup into one round trip.

15. SNI and ALPN — two tiny extensions that run the modern web

Two ClientHello extensions appeared repeatedly above; both exist to solve multi-tenancy, and both echo problems you have already met.

SNI (Server Name Indication). Part 6 showed many names resolving to one IP; Part 7's Host header showed how plain HTTP picks the right site on a shared server. But TLS has a bootstrap problem the Host header cannot solve: the server must present a certificate — the right certificate — before any HTTP exists, in its very first reply. Which of its 5,000 tenants' certs should a CDN edge present? SNI is the answer: the client puts the hostname it wants in plaintext in the ClientHello, and the server uses it to select the matching certificate. Every ALB (Application Load Balancer), CDN, and shared host depends on it — it is why one IP can serve a million HTTPS sites:

     │       sees this hostname)        │   ├ api.partner.net    ┤   this one
     │                                  │   └ blog.example.io    ┘     │
     │  ◄─── ServerHello + the cert for shop.example.com               │

The privacy nuance: SNI means an on-path observer (the coffee shop, the ISP, a national firewall) learns which hostname you are visiting even though everything else is encrypted — and indeed SNI is exactly what most network-level filters key on. The fix in deployment is ECH (Encrypted ClientHello): the real ClientHello is encrypted to a key published in DNS, hiding the hostname too — one line to remember, still rolling out.

ALPN (Application-Layer Protocol Negotiation). Part 7 ended with HTTP/2 — but how do client and server agree to speak h2 instead of HTTP/1.1 without a wasted round trip of asking? They piggyback on the handshake: the ClientHello lists protocols (h2, http/1.1), the server's reply picks one, and by the time the tunnel opens both sides already know what dialect flows inside it. In practice, HTTP/2 is only ever negotiated this way — browsers do not speak h2 over plaintext — so HTTPS is effectively the price of admission for HTTP/2's multiplexing. When you curl with -v and see ALPN: server accepted h2, this is what happened.

You now hold Parts 1–8. Stitch them together and narrate, step by step, what happens between pressing Enter and seeing a page — this is the walkthrough that interview question #1 ("what happens when you type a URL?") dreams of. Browser: fresh start, nothing cached. Network: 30 ms RTT to everything (Amazon has edges near you — Part 1's latency table).

  5. ServerHello. Amazon's endpoint picks TLS 1.3, TLS_AES_128_GCM_SHA256, returns its own key_share.
  6. Both ends compute the shared secret (Diffie–Hellman, §6) and derive handshake keys. Everything from here is ciphertext to the network.
  7. EncryptedExtensions: among them, ALPN verdict: h2 — the conversation inside will be HTTP/2 (Part 7).
  8. Certificate: leaf with SAN: www.amazon.com, amazon.com, … issued by a DigiCert intermediate, plus that intermediate (§10).

  15. Subresources: CSS, JS, images multiplex over the same connection (h2, Part 7) — no new handshakes for this origin.

🧪 What did the coffee-shop attacker see this time?

Replay section 1's eavesdropper against this connection. They observed: the DNS query for www.amazon.com (plaintext, unless you use encrypted DNS), the IP addresses and ports on every packet (Layer 3/4 headers cannot be encrypted — routers need them), the SNI hostname www.amazon.com in the ClientHello, and the sizes and timing of the encrypted records. They did not see: the path (/ or /order-history?), any header, any cookie, any body, or even the certificate (TLS 1.3 encrypts it). So an observer learns that you visited amazon.com and roughly how much you transferred — but nothing about what you did there. Metadata is visible; content is not. 17. TLS in YOUR backend — termination, dev certs, and debugging

Everything so far described the browser-to-server story. Now the part you own: where does TLS live in a backend architecture, and what do you do when it breaks at 2 a.m.?

17.1 Where to terminate TLS — the three patterns

"Terminating" TLS means being the endpoint that holds the private key, performs the handshake, and decrypts. Three placements cover essentially all real systems:

(a) Edge termination is the default you will meet everywhere: a load balancer or reverse proxy at the boundary holds the certificate, terminates TLS, and forwards plain HTTP to your apps over the private network (an AWS VPC — Virtual Private Cloud, Part 11). On AWS the certificate comes from ACM (AWS Certificate Manager) — free, validated via exactly the DNS challenge from §11.1, and auto-renewing forever, which deletes the Teams-outage class of §11.2 — attached to an ALB with a few clicks. The trade-offs, honestly: your apps never touch keystores (huge operational win), the LB can inspect, route on, and log the decrypted HTTP (Part 9 needs this for L7 routing), and handshake CPU is offloaded; in exchange, traffic between LB and app is plaintext, so you are trusting the VPC's network isolation — fine for most threat models, not for all.

(b) End-to-end TLS keeps ciphertext flowing to the app itself — required in regulated environments (payments, health) where "plaintext anywhere" fails audit. Spring Boot terminates TLS natively with a PKCS12 keystore (a password-protected bundle holding private key + cert chain):

# application.properties — Spring Boot serving HTTPS itself
server.port=8443
server.ssl.enabled=true
server.ssl.key-store=classpath:keystore.p12
server.ssl.key-store-type=PKCS12
server.ssl.key-store-password=${KEYSTORE_PASSWORD}
server.ssl.key-alias=api
# modern protocols only
server.ssl.enabled-protocols=TLSv1.3,TLSv1.2

(c) mTLS (mutual TLS) flips one default: in everything so far, only the server proved its identity (your browser never showed a certificate). In mTLS the server demands a client certificate too, and verifies it against a private CA — both sides authenticate. This is machine-to-machine identity: service A cryptographically is service A, no shared API key to leak. Managing certs for hundreds of services by hand is misery, which is why service meshes issue, rotate, and verify them automatically — that story, and zero-trust networking with it, is Part 13's.

17.2 Self-signed certificates in dev — and the right way to trust them

For localhost work you do not need a CA; you can sign your own certificate (you become a one-machine CA nobody trusts — yet):

# option 1: keytool (ships with the JDK) — straight into a PKCS12 keystore
keytool -genkeypair -alias dev -keyalg RSA -keysize 2048 \
  -storetype PKCS12 -keystore keystore.p12 -validity 365 \
  -dname "CN=localhost" -ext "SAN=dns:localhost,ip:127.0.0.1"

# option 2: openssl — key + cert as separate PEM files
openssl req -x509 -newkey rsa:2048 -nodes -days 365 \
  -keyout dev.key -out dev.crt -subj "/CN=localhost" \
  -addext "subjectAltName=DNS:localhost,IP:127.0.0.1"

Point your app at it and every client immediately complains — correctly: the chain ends at a root no trust store contains. The browser shows the "Your connection is not private" interstitial; curl says self-signed certificate and suggests -k. curl -k (and its cousins — verify=False in Python requests, rejectUnauthorized: false in Node, InsecureSkipVerify in Go) disables certificate validation entirely — acceptable for a one-off local poke, a disaster as a habit, because the same flag that accepts your dev cert accepts a MITM's. These flags have an unerring tendency to get committed and ride to production.

The right fix: leave validation on, and teach your trust store about your CA — the same mechanism the OS uses for the 150 real roots:

# JVM: import into cacerts (the JVM trust store) — Java 9+
keytool -importcert -alias local-dev -cacerts \
  -storepass changeit -file dev.crt
# (or keep a separate store and point the JVM at it:
#  -Djavax.net.ssl.trustStore=devtrust.p12)

# Node.js: append extra trusted CAs without touching code
NODE_EXTRA_CA_CERTS=/home/you/dev.crt node server.js

# or skip all of this: mkcert creates a local CA, installs it into your
# OS, browser, and Java trust stores, and issues localhost certs in 2 commands
mkcert -install
mkcert localhost 127.0.0.1

This distinction — disable validation vs extend trust — also explains the corporate-proxy mystery: when builds fail with unable to find valid certification path only on the office network, your company's TLS-inspecting proxy (a sanctioned MITM, §6 advanced) is re-signing traffic with its internal CA, and that CA is in your OS store but not in the JVM's cacerts or Node's bundle. Same fix: import the corporate root, don't disable validation.

17.4 HSTS and mixed content — the browser-side guardrails

HSTS (HTTP Strict Transport Security) closes the last plaintext gap. A user who types amazon.com gets one plain-HTTP request before the redirect to HTTPS — one chance for a MITM to intercept and keep them on attacker-controlled plaintext (the classic "SSL stripping" attack). The fix is a response header from Part 7's collection: Strict-Transport-Security: max-age=31536000; includeSubDomains — "browser, for the next year, refuse to speak plain HTTP to this domain at all, upgrade every URL to HTTPS before sending." First-visit exposure remains, so browsers ship a built-in preload list of domains that are HSTS from birth; you can submit yours.

And the one every React developer has met: mixed content. A page loaded over HTTPS may not quietly fetch over HTTP — an attacker who controls one plaintext script controls the whole "secure" page, so the browser blocks it:

Mixed Content: The page at 'https://app.example.com/' was loaded over
HTTPS, but requested an insecure XMLHttpRequest endpoint
'http://api.example.com/orders'. This request has been blocked; the
content must be served over HTTPS.

That console error is not a CORS problem and no fetch option fixes it: the API must be HTTPS (in dev, via the trust-store setup of §17.2; in prod, via the ALB of §17.1). Relative URLs and https:// absolute URLs in your React fetch calls prevent it by construction.

🟢 Beginner — say it simply

In real deployments the load balancer usually holds the certificate and "terminates" TLS; your app receives plain HTTP inside the private network. For local dev you sign your own certificate and teach your tools to trust it (don't just turn verification off). When TLS breaks, openssl s_client shows you exactly what the server presented and why it was rejected.

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

Patterns: edge termination (ACM cert on ALB, plaintext in VPC — operationally simplest, enables L7 routing), end-to-end (Spring Boot PKCS12 keystore via server.ssl.* — compliance), mTLS (both sides present certs — machine identity). Trust-store reality: JVM cacerts, Node's bundle + NODE_EXTRA_CA_CERTS, and OS stores are separate lists that drift. HSTS pins browsers to HTTPS; mixed-content blocking stops HTTPS pages calling HTTP APIs.

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

Termination choice is a threat-model decision: plaintext-in-VPC assumes network isolation holds, which zero-trust architectures explicitly refuse — hence mesh-issued mTLS everywhere (Part 13) with SPIFFE-style workload identities and certificates rotated hourly. Watch the operational sharp edges: ALBs re-encrypting to backends often skip hostname verification (know what your LB actually checks), session-ticket keys and OCSP stapling need rotation/monitoring, and every TLS-terminating hop is a place where client IP and protocol metadata must be re-conveyed (X-Forwarded-For and friends — Part 10's problem).

18. Interview questions

🎤 Try answering out loud before you click
Explain the TLS handshake to a junior engineer.

Frame it as three jobs: agree on algorithms, prove the server's identity, and create a shared secret key. The client opens with ClientHello ("here's what I can speak, here's the hostname I want — SNI — and here's my half of a key exchange"); the server answers with its choice, its certificate chain, its half of the key exchange, and a signature made with the certificate's private key proving it really owns that identity. The client verifies the chain up to a trusted root and checks the hostname, both sides combine the key-exchange halves (Diffie–Hellman) into the same session key, exchange Finished checksums of the whole conversation to detect tampering, and then switch to fast symmetric encryption (AES) for the actual HTTP. TLS 1.3 does all of that in one round trip; 1.2 took two.

How does the browser know the certificate is real and not forged?

It verifies a chain of digital signatures: the leaf certificate is signed by an intermediate CA, the intermediate by a root CA, and the root is pre-installed in the OS/browser trust store — trusted by fiat. Signatures are unforgeable without the signer's private key, and any modification breaks them. The browser additionally checks the validity window, that the URL's hostname appears in the SAN list, revocation (best-effort OCSP/CRL), algorithm strength, and Certificate Transparency proof. Crucially, the certificate alone proves nothing — anyone can replay a public cert — so the handshake also requires a fresh signature (CertificateVerify / signed key-exchange params) that only the holder of the matching private key can produce.

Symmetric vs asymmetric encryption — and why does TLS use both?

Symmetric uses one shared key for encrypt and decrypt (AES) — extremely fast, hardware-accelerated, but requires both parties to already share a secret, which strangers on a public network cannot do. Asymmetric uses a key pair — public key encrypts/verifies, private key decrypts/signs (RSA, elliptic curves) — solving first contact, but it is 100–1000× slower. TLS is a hybrid: asymmetric crypto runs briefly during the handshake to authenticate the server and agree on a session key; symmetric AES then encrypts the actual data. Scalpel for the introduction, conveyor belt for the conversation.

What is forward secrecy and why did TLS 1.3 make it mandatory?

Forward secrecy means past recorded traffic stays undecryptable even if the server's long-term private key is later stolen. It comes from ephemeral Diffie–Hellman: every connection derives its keys from fresh, throwaway key shares that are destroyed afterwards, so the certificate's key never encrypts anything — it only signs. Old TLS allowed static RSA key transport, where the session key was encrypted under the certificate's key; one key theft retroactively exposed years of recorded ciphertext. TLS 1.3 deleted that mode entirely: ephemeral (EC)DHE is the only option.

What changed between TLS 1.2 and TLS 1.3?

Speed: 1.3 finishes the full handshake in 1 RTT (vs 2) by sending key shares optimistically, and supports 0-RTT resumption (with a replay caveat limiting it to idempotent requests). Security: static RSA key exchange removed (forward secrecy mandatory), all legacy ciphers gone (only five AEAD suites remain), the certificate and most handshake messages are themselves encrypted, and downgrade protection is built in. Simplicity: a far smaller negotiation surface, which killed whole families of 1.2-era attacks.

What does the browser padlock NOT guarantee?

It does not vouch for the site's honesty, safety, or legal legitimacy — only that the connection is encrypted, integrity-protected, and terminates at a server that proved control of the domain in the address bar. A phishing site at a look-alike domain can carry a perfectly valid free certificate and show the same padlock. It also does not hide that you visited the site (DNS, SNI, and IPs are visible to the network) and says nothing about what the site does with your data after it arrives.

Where would you terminate TLS in a typical AWS architecture, and why?

Default: at the edge — an ALB with a free, auto-renewing ACM certificate — then plain HTTP to services inside the VPC. This centralizes certificate management, removes keystores from apps, offloads handshake CPU, and lets the load balancer route on decrypted HTTP (paths, headers — needed for L7 routing). Mention the trade-off: plaintext inside the VPC means trusting network isolation, so compliance-heavy or zero-trust environments re-encrypt to the backends (end-to-end TLS) or run mesh-issued mTLS between services. The strong answer presents it as a threat-model decision, not a single right answer.

What is SNI and why do multi-tenant platforms need it?

Server Name Indication: the client states the hostname it wants inside the ClientHello, in plaintext, before the server must choose a certificate. Without it, a server could present only one certificate per IP address — impossible for CDNs, ALBs, and shared hosts serving thousands of domains from one IP (the TLS-era version of Part 7's Host header, which arrives too late because it is inside the encrypted channel). Privacy note: SNI leaks the hostname to on-path observers; Encrypted ClientHello (ECH) is the emerging fix.

What is mTLS and when would you use it?

Mutual TLS: both sides present and verify certificates, not just the server — so the client is cryptographically authenticated too. Use it for machine-to-machine identity: service-to-service calls in a microservices fleet, partner API integrations, IoT devices — anywhere "which workload is calling me?" must be answered without bearer secrets that can leak. Operationally it demands a private CA plus issuance and rotation automation, which is exactly what service meshes provide out of the box (sidecars handle mTLS transparently).

Decode TLS_ECDHE_RSA_WITH_AES_128_GCM_SHA256.

Four segments: ECDHE — key agreement via Elliptic-Curve Diffie–Hellman, Ephemeral, so the session has forward secrecy; RSA — the server authenticates with an RSA signature (its certificate holds an RSA key); AES_128_GCM — the bulk data is encrypted with 128-bit AES in GCM mode, an AEAD that encrypts and authenticates in one pass; SHA256 — the hash used for key derivation and handshake integrity. Bonus: explain why TLS 1.3 names are shorter (TLS_AES_128_GCM_SHA256) — key exchange and authentication are negotiated separately there.

How does Let's Encrypt verify you own a domain before issuing a certificate?

Via ACME challenges proving domain control: HTTP-01 — serve a CA-chosen token at /.well-known/acme-challenge/ on the domain, proving you control the web server the name points to; or DNS-01 — publish a CA-chosen value in a TXT record at _acme-challenge.domain, proving you control the DNS zone (required for wildcards). The client (certbot, Caddy, cert-manager) automates challenge, issuance, installation, and — since certs last 90 days — renewal. CAA records constrain which CAs may issue at all, and every issued cert lands in public Certificate Transparency logs.

Production is throwing TLS errors right now. Walk me through your debugging.

First classify the error: expired, hostname mismatch, untrusted chain, or protocol failure — then go look at reality with openssl s_client -connect host:443 -servername host. Check the Verify return code (0 ok; 10 expired; 21 missing intermediate; 18 self-signed), the chain block for whether the server sends leaf + intermediates, the SANs and dates via -showcerts + openssl x509 -noout -dates. Classic causes in rough order: certificate expired (renewal automation broke), incomplete chain (works in Chrome, fails in Java/curl), SNI mismatch (client not sending the right hostname, wrong cert returned), trust-store gaps (JVM cacerts vs OS, corporate MITM proxy), and clock skew on the client. The discipline: never "fix" it with verification-off flags.

19. Common misconceptions

❌ Myths engineers actually believe

❌ "HTTPS hides which site I'm visiting."
✅ The network still sees your DNS lookups, the destination IP on every packet, and the hostname in plaintext SNI — observers (ISPs, firewalls) know that you visited amazon.com and when. What HTTPS hides is the content: paths, query strings, headers, cookies, bodies. Encrypted DNS and ECH narrow the metadata leak but the IP always shows.

❌ "The padlock means the site is trustworthy."
✅ The padlock means the connection is secure to whoever controls that domain. Phishing sites use valid HTTPS routinely — certificates are free and automated. The padlock cannot evaluate intent; check the domain name itself.

❌ "Certificates encrypt the traffic."
✅ Certificates authenticate — they bind a public key to a name so you know who you exchanged keys with. The encryption keys come from ephemeral Diffie–Hellman during the handshake, and the bulk encryption is symmetric AES. In TLS 1.3 the certificate's key never encrypts anything at all; it only signs.

❌ "Asymmetric crypto encrypts the whole session."
✅ It is 100–1000× too slow for that. Asymmetric operations appear only in the handshake (authentication signature, key agreement); 99.9%+ of the bytes are encrypted with symmetric AES-GCM at hardware speed. TLS is hybrid by design.

❌ "SSL and TLS are different live protocols — and an 'SSL certificate' is a special kind."
✅ SSL (2.0/3.0) is dead and prohibited; TLS is its renamed successor and the only thing running. "SSL certificate," "SSL termination," and OpenSSL are fossil vocabulary. Certificates themselves are protocol-version-agnostic X.509 documents — there is no SSL cert vs TLS cert.

❌ "TLS is too slow / too expensive to run everywhere."
✅ A 2010 belief. AES-NI makes bulk encryption ~1–2% CPU; TLS 1.3 costs one round trip once, and session resumption plus connection reuse amortize even that to near zero. Google reported single-digit-percent overhead enabling HTTPS for Gmail back in 2010 — on today's hardware it is noise. The web is ~95% HTTPS precisely because the cost argument died.

❌ "A valid certificate means the company is legitimate."
✅ A DV certificate proves exactly one fact: control of a domain name at issuance time, verified by an automated file-or-TXT-record challenge. No human checked a business license. OV/EV certs add organization vetting, but browsers no longer visually distinguish them — so no user can tell. Legitimacy is simply not a property the certificate system asserts.

❌ "We're behind a load balancer that does HTTPS, so encryption is someone else's problem."
✅ Termination at the edge is a decision, with a consequence you own: plaintext between the LB and your app. Know whether your threat model accepts that, whether compliance does, and what headers (X-Forwarded-Proto) tell your app it was originally HTTPS — Spring Security and cookie Secure flags depend on getting that right.
