For ten parts you have built a mental model of real networking: IP addresses and CIDR blocks, routers and routing tables, TCP, ports, firewalls, DNS, load balancers. 🧭 Why cloud networking exists in this shape

Networking is the one part of the cloud that cannot be a thin abstraction, because packets are physical (Part 1: the Internet is glass under oceans). When a thousand companies share Amazon's data centers, each one needs the illusion that it owns a private, isolated network — its own address space, its own firewall rules, its own routers — even though their traffic rides the same physical fiber and the same physical switches as everyone else's. The entire VPC system exists to deliver that illusion: software-defined networking (SDN) that gives you a private virtual network carved out of shared hardware, configured entirely through API calls instead of a screwdriver and a cable. You are renting a slice of a router, not a router.

📜 What came before — racking iron and waiting weeks

Before AWS, "I need a new server behind a load balancer in a private network segment" was a multi-week capital project, and most of the work was networking. You filed a request; weeks later a server arrived on a pallet. Someone racked it (bolted it into a rack), ran power, and ran a physical Ethernet cable from its NIC (Part 1) to a port on a top-of-rack switch. A network engineer configured a VLAN (Virtual LAN — a way to slice one physical switch into isolated broadcast domains, the ancestor of the subnet) so your app servers couldn't see the finance department's. A separate hardware firewall appliance (a Cisco ASA or a Juniper, costing tens of thousands of dollars) was cabled in and its rules typed by hand. Adding a load balancer meant buying an F5 BIG-IP box and cabling it too. Want a second server for redundancy? Repeat everything, and pray you cabled the redundant path correctly. Lead time: weeks to months. Mistakes meant a site visit. AWS's insight was that all of that is information, not hardware — a VLAN is a table entry, a firewall rule is a row, a route is a row — so it virtualized every one of those boxes into an API. A VPC with subnets, route tables, an internet gateway, a NAT gateway, security groups, and a load balancer is now a 60-second terraform apply. That is the entire revolution: weeks of cabling collapsed into a few hundred lines of declarative config.

🌍 Analogy — your private office inside a giant shared skyscraper

Picture AWS as an enormous skyscraper (a Region) that thousands of companies rent space in. Your VPC is your company's private, isolated office inside that building: you get your own internal address scheme for desks, your own locked doors, and a guarantee that the company two floors up cannot wander into your space — even though you share the building's foundation, elevators, and electrical grid. We will grow this analogy through the whole chapter: subnets are floors of your office; the internet gateway is the lobby door to the public street; the NAT gateway is the staff-only side exit your people can leave through but no outsider can enter; security groups are bouncers standing at each individual room checking each person's badge; and NACLs are the rules posted at the entrance to each floor. Keep this building in your head — every AWS networking concept slots into it.

One discipline for this whole chapter, because it is what separates engineers who "use AWS" from engineers who understand it: every time a new term appears, silently translate it back to Parts 1–10. "This is the CIDR math from Part 3." "This is the L4 firewall from Parts 4–5." "This is the ALB from Part 9." If you keep doing that translation, AWS networking stops being a pile of new acronyms and becomes a thin, familiar skin over networking you already own.

🟢 Beginner — say it simply

AWS networking is normal networking — IP addresses, routers, firewalls — except you set it all up by clicking buttons or running code instead of plugging in cables. Nothing about the underlying TCP/IP changed. Amazon just turned the hardware into software you rent.

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

AWS implements software-defined networking: virtual routers, virtual firewalls, and virtual network segments multiplexed onto shared physical hardware, configured via API. A VPC is your isolated address space; subnets, route tables, gateways, and security groups are the virtualized equivalents of VLANs, routing tables, edge routers, and firewall appliances. The packets are ordinary IP packets (Part 3) carrying ordinary TCP segments (Part 4).

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

The illusion is delivered by an SDN data plane (AWS's Nitro system offloads VPC encapsulation to dedicated hardware on each host). Your packets are wrapped in an overlay encapsulation (a mapping layer the Mapping Service maintains) so that two VPCs can both use 10.0.0.0/16 without colliding on the physical network — overlapping private ranges (Part 3, RFC 1918) are fine because they are isolated by the overlay, which is also why peering two VPCs with overlapping CIDRs is impossible. Security groups and routing are enforced in that data plane, not by a box in the path, which is why they scale horizontally and have no single bottleneck.

2. Regions and Availability Zones — geography is destiny

Everything in AWS lives somewhere on Earth, and Part 1 already told you why that matters: latency is bounded by distance and the speed of light (~200 km/ms in fiber), and "the cloud" is physical buildings. AWS organizes its physical footprint into two nested units you must understand before you can place a single subnet.

2.1 A Region is a geographic area

A Region is a named geographic area containing a cluster of AWS data centers — for example us-east-1 (Northern Virginia), eu-west-1 (Ireland), or ap-south-1 (Mumbai). When you "deploy to AWS," you first choose a Region, and that choice is a Part 1 latency decision: put your servers near your users. An ap-south-1 deployment serves Mumbai users in single-digit milliseconds and US East Coast users in ~200 ms (Part 1's table). Regions are almost completely isolated from each other — separate power, separate networks — and most AWS resources (VPCs, subnets, instances) live in exactly one Region. Cross-region traffic is WAN traffic (Part 1): expensive, higher latency, and crosses the public backbone or AWS's private inter-region links.

2.2 An Availability Zone is one (or more) discrete data centers

Inside a Region, an AZ (Availability Zone) is one or more discrete data centers with independent power, cooling, and physical networking. A Region like ap-south-1 has three AZs (ap-south-1a, 1b, 1c). The defining property: an AZ is a failure domain. A power event, a flood, a fire, a network partition in one AZ should not take down another AZ. Crucially, AZs within a Region are connected to each other by AWS-owned, low-latency, high-bandwidth private fiber — single-digit-millisecond round trips, exactly the MAN-like behavior Part 1 described ("a Region behaves like a MAN — multiple data centers in one metro area, linked by AWS-owned fiber").

🧭 Why multi-AZ is the fundamental availability unit

Here is the single most important operational fact in this entire chapter, and the reason every diagram you will draw has things duplicated side by side: an AZ can fail, and AWS designs for it, so you must too. A real AWS outage is usually "AZ us-east-1b is having trouble," not "AWS is down." If your database and all your app servers live in one AZ, that AZ's bad afternoon is your outage. The fundamental availability pattern in AWS is therefore: spread your system across 2 or 3 AZs, so the loss of any one AZ leaves a healthy copy running. Multi-AZ is not a premium feature you add later — it is the baseline shape of a correct deployment. Almost every "why is it built this way?" question in this chapter resolves to "so that one AZ can fail without taking you down."

🌍 Analogy — Regions are cities, AZs are buildings on different power grids

A Region is a city (Mumbai). The AZs are three office buildings in that city, deliberately placed far enough apart to be on different power substations and flood plains, but close enough that a courier can run between them in a couple of minutes (that's the low-latency private fiber). You rent desks in all three buildings so that if one building loses power, your business keeps running from the other two. Putting all your staff in one building to "keep them close" is exactly the mistake of a single-AZ deployment.

🟢 Beginner — say it simply

A Region is a city where AWS has data centers (Mumbai, Virginia, Ireland). An Availability Zone is one data center building in that city, with its own power. You spread your app across two or three buildings so that if one loses power, the others keep your app running.

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

Regions are isolated geographic clusters; AZs are isolated failure domains within a Region, linked by low-latency private fiber (~1 ms). Resources are Region-scoped or AZ-scoped. Cross-AZ traffic is fast but metered; cross-region traffic is WAN-class latency and cost. High availability means deploying redundant capacity in ≥2 AZs and letting a load balancer (Part 9) route around a failed one.

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

AZ identifiers like us-east-1a are randomized per account (your 1a may be my 1c) to spread load; use AZ IDs (use1-az1) when you need a stable cross-account reference. AZs are correlated-failure boundaries: the design assumption is independence, but control-plane dependencies can still cause multi-AZ events (the famous large us-east-1 incidents). Quorum systems (3-AZ deployments for etcd, ZooKeeper, RDS Multi-AZ) exist precisely so that losing one AZ leaves a majority — this is the same WAN-boundary math from Part 1 applied at metro scale.

3. The VPC — your own virtual network

Now the centerpiece construct. A VPC (Virtual Private Cloud) is your own logically isolated virtual network inside an AWS Region. Part 1 made you a promise — "an AWS VPC is your virtual LAN" — and this is where we deliver it. A VPC is a software-defined LAN/WAN that you own: you pick its address range, you carve it into subnets, you write its routing and firewall rules, and nothing gets in or out until you wire it up.

3.1 You pick its CIDR block

The first decision when you create a VPC is its CIDR block — and this is the exact CIDR math from Part 3, no new concept. You choose a private RFC 1918 range (Part 3 explained why private: these addresses are reusable and not routable on the public Internet, so every company can use 10.0.0.0/16 inside their own VPC without colliding). The convention this whole course uses:

VPC CIDR:  10.0.0.0/16

  /16  means the first 16 bits are fixed (10.0.) and the last 16 bits vary.
  addresses:  10.0.0.0  through  10.0.255.255
  count:      2^(32-16) = 2^16 = 65,536 IP addresses

  this is exactly the Part 3 calculation:
    host bits = 32 minus 16 = 16   then   2^16 = 65,536 addresses

Sixty-five thousand addresses is a generous home for a serious application. Why so big? Because you will slice it into subnets, and you want room for each tier in each AZ, plus headroom to grow (Part 12: every Kubernetes pod will consume a VPC IP, so you can burn addresses fast). AWS lets a VPC CIDR be anywhere from /16 (65,536 addresses) to /28 (16 addresses); /16 is the standard starting choice.

🔑 Key point — one VPC spans the whole Region

A VPC is Region-wide: a single VPC stretches across all the Availability Zones in its Region. You do not create a VPC per AZ. You create one VPC for the Region, and then you place subnets inside it, each subnet pinned to one AZ. This is the inverse of a common beginner belief that a VPC lives in one AZ — it does not. The VPC is the building; the AZs are floors-with-independent-power that the building spans.

3.2 Default isolation — nothing in or out until you wire it

A freshly created VPC is a sealed room. Instances inside it can talk to each other (there is an automatic local route — section 5), but there is no path to the Internet in either direction until you add the components in sections 6 and 7. This default-deny posture is a security feature, and it maps to the analogy: your private office in the skyscraper has no door to the street until you install one. Everything in this chapter after the VPC is, in some sense, you deciding exactly which doors to cut and who may pass through them.

🟢 Beginner — say it simply

A VPC is your own private network inside AWS, like your home Wi-Fi network but virtual and bigger. You pick its range of IP addresses (we use 10.0.0.0/16, which is 65,536 addresses). By default nothing can reach it from the Internet until you deliberately open a door.

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

A VPC is a Region-wide, software-defined L3 network with an admin-chosen RFC 1918 CIDR. It provides isolation (overlapping private ranges across VPCs are fine), an implicit local route for intra-VPC reachability, and default-deny to the Internet. You subdivide it into per-AZ subnets and attach gateways, route tables, and firewalls to define connectivity.

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

Plan CIDR ranges before you build, because changing them later is painful: VPC CIDRs cannot overlap if you ever want to peer or attach to a Transit Gateway (§12), and IPv4 exhaustion inside a VPC (Part 12's pods) forces secondary CIDR blocks. The VPC is also where you decide IPv6 (dual-stack), DNS resolution/hostnames settings (which make Route 53 private zones and internal service discovery work — Part 13), and whether to use a custom DHCP option set. Treat the address plan as an API contract: it is the hardest thing to change after services depend on it.

4. Subnets — slicing the VPC, one per AZ

A VPC's /16 is too big to be useful as one flat network. You slice it into subnets, and this is precisely the subnetting worked example Part 3 promised you: take 10.0.0.0/16 and split it into /24 pieces. Each /24 is a subnet with 256 addresses (Part 3: host bits = 32 − 24 = 8, so 2⁸ = 256).

Splitting the VPC  10.0.0.0/16  into /24 subnets (the Part 3 promise, delivered):

  10.0.0.0/24    addresses 10.0.0.0   - 10.0.0.255    (256 addrs)
  10.0.1.0/24    addresses 10.0.1.0   - 10.0.1.255    (256 addrs)
  10.0.10.0/24   addresses 10.0.10.0  - 10.0.10.255   (256 addrs)
  10.0.11.0/24   addresses 10.0.11.0  - 10.0.11.255   (256 addrs)
  10.0.20.0/24   addresses 10.0.20.0  - 10.0.20.255   (256 addrs)
  ... up to 10.0.255.0/24  ──►  256 possible /24 subnets in one /16

  the third octet (0, 1, 10, 11, 20) is just a label we chose to keep
  tiers readable. nothing magic about the numbers.
🔑 Key point — a subnet lives in exactly ONE Availability Zone

This is the rule that forces the shape of every AWS diagram. A VPC spans all AZs, but a subnet is pinned to exactly one AZ and never spans two. So if you want your app tier present in two AZs (and you do — §2: any AZ can fail), you create two app subnets: one in AZ-a, one in AZ-b. This is why real architectures show subnets in mirrored pairs: each tier is duplicated, once per AZ, for high availability.

4.1 The crucial distinction: public vs private subnet

Every AWS tutorial talks about "public subnets" and "private subnets," and here is the honest truth that the console's friendly UI obscures: there is no "public" checkbox that makes a subnet public. A subnet is not public because of a setting on the subnet. A subnet is public for exactly one reason, which §5 makes rigorous:

🔑 The single rule that defines public vs private

A subnet is public if and only if its route table has a route sending 0.0.0.0/0 (all non-local traffic) to an Internet Gateway. A subnet is private if its route table has no such route (it sends 0.0.0.0/0 to a NAT gateway, or nowhere). "Public" and "private" are descriptions of the route table, not properties of the subnet. Burn this in now; it is the most-misunderstood fact in AWS networking and the answer to an interview question you will be asked.

🟢 Beginner — say it simply

A subnet is a slice of your VPC's addresses, and each slice lives in one data center (AZ). You make "public" subnets for the load balancer (which the Internet can reach) and "private" subnets for your app and database (which it can't). You duplicate each slice in two AZs so a data-center failure doesn't take you down.

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

Subnets are CIDR sub-ranges of the VPC, each bound to one AZ. "Public" vs "private" is determined entirely by the associated route table's 0.0.0.0/0 target (IGW = public, NAT/none = private), not by any subnet flag. The standard secure layout is three tiers (public LB, private app, private data), each mirrored per AZ for HA. AWS reserves 5 addresses in every subnet (first four + last), so a /24 yields 251 usable.

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

Right-size subnets to the workload: EKS with the VPC CNI assigns a VPC IP per pod, so app subnets often need to be larger than /24 or you exhaust addresses and pods fail to schedule (Part 12). The five reserved addresses per subnet are the network address, VPC router (.1), the Amazon-provided DNS resolver at base+2 (.2 — important for §12's endpoints and Part 6's DNS), a reserved .3, and the broadcast address. Keep data subnets with no route to IGW or NAT for defense in depth; reach AWS services from them via VPC endpoints (§12) instead.

5. Route tables — the mechanism that defines public vs private

This is the section that makes "public subnet" stop being magic. A route table is a set of rules that says, for a given destination IP range, where to send the packet next. It is exactly the routing table from Part 3 — longest-prefix match and all — now attached to subnets.

OSI Layer 3 (Network) — route tables forward by destination IP, the L3 job from Part 3

Every subnet is associated with exactly one route table (multiple subnets can share one). When a packet leaves an instance, AWS consults that subnet's route table and applies longest-prefix match (Part 3): the most specific matching route wins. Two routes matter most.

5.1 The local route — why intra-VPC traffic "just works"

Every route table is born with one route you cannot remove: the local route for the VPC's own CIDR. It is what makes any instance in the VPC able to reach any other instance in the VPC, across subnets and across AZs, with no configuration. This is the implicit "everything inside the building can reach everything else inside the building" rule.

Every route table contains this, automatically and permanently:

  Destination        Target      Meaning
  ---------------    --------    --------------------------------------
  10.0.0.0/16        local       "for any address inside THIS VPC,
                                  deliver it directly within the VPC"

  this is why your Spring Boot app in 10.0.10.0/24 can reach RDS in
  10.0.20.0/24 with zero routing config — both match the local route.

5.2 The default route — the 0.0.0.0/0 entry that decides everything

The default route, destination 0.0.0.0/0, matches everything not already matched by a more specific route (Part 3: it is the lowest-priority catch-all, the "default gateway"). Its target is the single bit that makes a subnet public or private. Compare the two route tables side by side — this is the heart of the chapter:

🔑 Key point — the route table IS the definition of "public"

Look at the three tables above. The subnets are identical kinds of objects. The only difference between "public," "private-with-egress," and "private-isolated" is one row: where 0.0.0.0/0 points. Internet Gateway → public. NAT gateway → private-but-can-go-out. No default route → fully isolated. When someone asks "what makes a subnet public?", the entire correct answer is: a route to an Internet Gateway for 0.0.0.0/0 in its route table (plus a public IP on the instance, §6). 🟢 Beginner — say it simply

A route table is a list of directions: "to reach these addresses, go this way." Every subnet uses one. The local line lets everything inside your VPC talk to everything else. The 0.0.0.0/0 line means "everything else (the Internet)" — and whether it points to the Internet Gateway or the NAT gateway is literally what makes the subnet public or private.

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

Route tables forward by destination CIDR using longest-prefix match (Part 3). The immutable local route (VPC-CIDR → local) provides intra-VPC reachability across subnets/AZs. The default route (0.0.0.0/0) target classifies the subnet: IGW = public, NAT gateway = private-egress, absent = isolated. Subnet-to-route-table association is the unit of policy; subnets without an explicit association use the VPC's main route table.

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

Beyond IGW/NAT, route targets include VPC peering connections (pcx-), Transit Gateway attachments (tgw-), VPC endpoints (gateway endpoints inject prefix-list routes for S3/DynamoDB, §12), virtual private gateways (Site-to-Site VPN), and egress-only IGWs (IPv6). Longest-prefix match lets you layer specific overrides (a /32 blackhole route, or a more-specific route to a peered VPC) above the catch-all. Misrouted 0.0.0.0/0 is the single most common cause of "my private instance can unexpectedly reach the Internet" (it was placed in a public subnet) or "my app can't pull dependencies" (no NAT route) — always read the effective route table for the instance's subnet first.

6. The Internet Gateway — the VPC's door to the world

The IGW (Internet Gateway) is the component that connects your VPC to the public Internet. You create one, attach it to the VPC, and point public subnets' 0.0.0.0/0 route at it (§5). It is the lobby door of your office building.

OSI Layer 3 (Network) — the IGW routes packets and performs 1:1 NAT on public IPs

6.1 It is not a bottleneck and not a single point of failure

A beginner worries: "if all my Internet traffic goes through one gateway, isn't that a bottleneck or a single point of failure?" No. The IGW is a horizontally scaled, redundant, fully managed AWS component — it is not a box sitting in a rack that can fall over. AWS implements it in the SDN fabric across the Region; it imposes no bandwidth limit and has no AZ affinity. You attach exactly one IGW per VPC and never think about its capacity again. (Contrast this with the NAT gateway in §7, which is per-AZ and does have throughput characteristics and cost.)

6.2 The IGW also performs the public-IP NAT

Here is the Part 3 NAT callback. Instances inside the VPC have private addresses (10.0.x.x). The public Internet cannot route to a 10.x address (Part 3: RFC 1918 ranges are non-routable publicly). So how does an instance with a public IP communicate? The IGW performs 1:1 NAT (Network Address Translation): it maintains a mapping between the instance's private IP and its assigned public IP (or Elastic IP), rewriting the source address on the way out and the destination address on the way in. This is the exact NAT mechanism from Part 3, done by the gateway.

🔑 Key point — "public" requires BOTH a route AND a public IP

A subnet is truly Internet-reachable only when two conditions hold together: (1) its route table sends 0.0.0.0/0 to the IGW (§5), and (2) the instance actually has a public IP or Elastic IP for the IGW to translate. A "public subnet" instance with no public IP can still be reached inbound by nothing, and reaches out by nothing, because the IGW has no public address to map. Both pieces, or no Internet.

🟢 Beginner — say it simply

The Internet Gateway is the door between your VPC and the public Internet. It also translates your instance's private address (10.0.x.x) to a public one the Internet understands, just like your home router does. AWS runs it redundantly, so it never becomes a bottleneck.

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

One IGW attaches per VPC; it is a horizontally scaled managed component with no bandwidth cap or AZ affinity. It provides Internet routing for public subnets (target of 0.0.0.0/0) and performs static 1:1 NAT between an instance's private IP and its public/Elastic IP (Part 3). Internet reachability requires both the IGW route and an assigned public IP.

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

The IGW's NAT is stateless 1:1 address mapping, distinct from the NAT gateway's stateful port-address translation (§7). Auto-assigned public IPv4 changes on stop/start; an Elastic IP (§12) is the stable alternative. For IPv6, addresses are globally routable so there is no NAT — you use an egress-only Internet Gateway to get outbound-only semantics. AWS now charges for all public IPv4 addresses, which (with the NAT gateway's cost) is pushing architectures toward fewer public IPs, IPv6, and VPC endpoints.

7. The NAT Gateway — outbound-only for private subnets

Your Spring Boot servers live in private subnets and have no public IP — good, the Internet cannot reach them. But they still need to initiate outbound connections: pull container images from a registry, download Maven/npm dependencies, call the Stripe API, fetch secrets, reach an S3 bucket. How does a machine with no public address reach out? The NAT Gateway (NAT GW) is the answer, and it is the home-router NAT from Part 3, delivered as a managed AWS service.

OSI Layers 3–4 — NAT rewrites IP addresses (L3) and tracks ports for the connection (L4)

7.1 Outbound yes, inbound no — exactly the home-router model

Part 3 taught you that home NAT lets every device on your LAN reach the Internet through one public IP, while blocking unsolicited inbound connections (nobody on the Internet can open a connection to your laptop behind the router). The NAT gateway does precisely this for a private subnet: private instances make outbound connections through it, the NAT gateway's public/Elastic IP appears as the source to the outside world, and return traffic for those connections is allowed back — but no one outside can initiate a connection inward, because there is no inbound mapping until the inside starts the conversation.

7.2 It lives in a public subnet; private route tables point at it

The wiring is the elegant part. The NAT gateway itself sits in a public subnet (so it can reach the IGW), and the private subnets' route tables send 0.0.0.0/0 to the NAT gateway (§5). So an outbound packet from a private app server flows: private EC2 → NAT GW (in the public subnet) → IGW → Internet, and the response retraces the path back. One NAT gateway per AZ is the HA pattern (if the NAT gateway lived in only AZ-a, an AZ-a failure would cut off AZ-b's outbound traffic — so you deploy one per AZ and point each AZ's private route table at its own AZ's NAT gateway).

🧭 Why this design is the security win

This is the whole reason your app servers stay private. They get everything they need from the outside world (dependencies, APIs, secrets) without ever being reachable from it. An attacker scanning the public Internet finds your ALB (which only speaks HTTPS) and nothing else — your Spring Boot servers and your database are simply not addressable from outside. Outbound-without-inbound is the asymmetry (Part 1: who initiates) that makes private subnets safe yet functional.

🧪 Real example — the famous NAT gateway bill surprise

The NAT gateway is one of the most notorious lines on a surprise AWS bill, for two reasons: an hourly charge plus a per-gigabyte data processing charge on everything that flows through it. The classic horror story: a team routes large S3 transfers or cross-service traffic through the NAT gateway by accident and gets a five-figure data-processing bill. Two fixes you should know:

  • For S3 and DynamoDB, use a gateway VPC endpoint (§12) — traffic skips the NAT gateway entirely and the data-processing charge with it, and it's free.
  • Don't over-provision: one NAT gateway per AZ is the pattern, but a dev environment can share a single one (trading some HA for cost).

The cost lesson connects to Part 1: data has a price proportional to how far and through how much infrastructure it travels. Keep chatty traffic local and off the NAT path.

Your private servers have no public address, so the Internet can't reach them — but they still need to download things and call other services. The NAT gateway is a one-way door: your servers can go out through it, but nothing outside can come in. It's the same thing your home router does for your laptop.

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

A NAT gateway lives in a public subnet with an Elastic IP; private route tables target it for 0.0.0.0/0. It performs stateful source NAT (PAT) so many private instances share one public IP for outbound flows, and only return traffic for established flows is allowed back — no inbound initiation. Deploy one per AZ for HA. Watch the hourly + per-GB data-processing cost; use gateway endpoints for S3/DynamoDB to bypass it.

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

A NAT gateway supports up to ~55,000 simultaneous connections per unique destination (more across many destinations) and can exhaust ports under extreme fan-out to a single endpoint — a real failure mode for chatty egress to one API. It is AZ-scoped: route each private subnet to a NAT gateway in its own AZ to keep the path same-AZ and avoid cross-AZ data charges (and to survive an AZ loss). NAT gateways do not work for inbound or for protocols that embed addresses without ALG support; for private inbound from on-prem use a different path (VPN/Direct Connect, §12). The cost model makes VPC endpoints and same-AZ design financially, not just architecturally, important.

8. Security Groups — the stateful, identity-aware firewall

Now the firewalls, and this is where Parts 4 and 5 become concrete. A Security Group (SG) is a virtual firewall you attach to a resource — an ENI (Elastic Network Interface, the virtual NIC of an instance), an EC2 instance, an ECS task, an ALB, an RDS database. It controls which traffic, by protocol and port and source, is allowed in and out.

OSI Layers 3–4 — SGs filter by IP (L3) and by port/protocol (L4) — the firewall from Parts 4–5

Why L3/L4? Because an SG rule is written in terms of an IP range (Layer 3, Part 3) and a port + protocol (Layer 4, Parts 4–5): "allow TCP 443 from 0.0.0.0/0." It does not understand HTTP paths or headers — that's Layer 7, the ALB's job (§11). An SG is the stateful packet filter from Parts 4–5, attached to your resource and configured by API.

8.1 Three properties that define how SGs behave

(1) Allow-only — there is no "deny." An SG contains only allow rules. You cannot write "deny traffic from this IP" in a security group. The evaluation is simply: is there any rule that allows this packet? If yes, it passes; if no rule matches, it is dropped (implicit deny). This surprises people coming from traditional firewalls — to block something with a security group, you simply don't allow it. (NACLs in §9 are where explicit deny lives.)

(2) Stateful — return traffic is automatic. This is the Part 4 connection-state callback, and it is the most important behavioral difference from NACLs. A security group is stateful: if you allow an inbound connection, the response is automatically allowed back out, and vice versa, because the SG tracks the state of each connection (Part 4: TCP connections have state — the handshake, established, etc.). You allow inbound TCP 443; when your server replies, that reply flows out without you writing any outbound rule, because the SG remembers "this is the return half of an allowed connection." You never write rules for return traffic.

(3) SG rules can reference other SGs — the killer feature. A source or destination in an SG rule does not have to be an IP range. It can be another security group's ID. "Allow TCP 8080 from sg-alb" means "allow port 8080 from anything that has the ALB's security group attached" — with no IP addresses anywhere. This is identity-based, IP-free microsegmentation: you describe the role of the allowed source, not its address. When the ALB scales out and adds nodes with new private IPs, your rule keeps working because it references the group, not the addresses.

🌍 Analogy — security groups are bouncers checking each person's badge

A security group is a bouncer standing at the door of one specific room (one instance), checking every person trying to enter. The bouncer has a list of who's allowed in — but the list isn't names and addresses, it's badges: "anyone wearing an ALB badge may enter on port 8080." It doesn't matter how many ALB staff there are or what their employee numbers are; the badge is the credential. And the bouncer is stateful: once it lets someone in, it remembers them and lets them walk back out without re-checking. The implicit-deny rule is just "no badge on my list? you don't get in" — there's no separate "banned" list at the room door (that's the floor-level NACL's job, §9).

8.2 The three-tier SG chain — the most important practical diagram

Here is how the identity-based feature builds a complete, IP-free security model for our Spring Boot deployment. Three security groups, each referencing the one in front of it, form a chain where every tier can only be reached by the tier that should reach it:

  INBOUND

The ALB is the only thing the public can reach, and only on 443. There is not a single IP address in the app or db rules — if you add a third app server or replace the database, the rules need no edits. 🟢 Beginner — say it simply

A security group is a firewall around one server (or the load balancer, or the database). It only has "allow" rules — to block something, you just don't allow it. It's smart about replies: if you allow a request in, the answer goes back out automatically. The best trick: a rule can say "allow the app servers" by name instead of by IP address.

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

SGs are stateful, allow-only L3/L4 filters attached to ENIs (instances, ALB, RDS). Inbound and outbound rules are evaluated as "does any rule permit this?"; return traffic for established connections is auto-permitted (stateful, Part 4). Rules can reference other SGs as source/destination, enabling identity-based microsegmentation that survives scaling and IP churn. The canonical pattern is a tiered chain: ALB-SG ⟵ public, app-SG ⟵ ALB-SG, db-SG ⟵ app-SG.

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

SG references resolve in the data plane to the current member ENIs' addresses, updated automatically — this is how they track autoscaling. SGs are evaluated per-ENI, so a multi-homed instance can have different rules per interface. There are account limits (rules per SG, SGs per ENI) that matter at scale. Because SGs are allow-only and stateful, they cannot express "block this specific bad IP" — that requires a NACL deny (§9) or AWS Network Firewall / WAF. Over-broad egress (0.0.0.0/0 on all ports) is a common finding; least-privilege egress (only 443 + the DB port) limits blast radius and data exfiltration paths.

9. NACLs — the stateless wall at the subnet boundary

The second firewall operates at a different level. A NACL (Network Access Control List) is a firewall attached to a subnet, filtering every packet that crosses the subnet boundary in or out. Where a security group wraps one resource, a NACL wraps a whole floor.

OSI Layers 3–4 — NACLs filter by IP (L3) and port/protocol (L4) at the subnet edge

9.1 Three properties — and they are nearly the opposite of an SG's

(1) Stateless — you must allow both directions. This is the headline difference and the classic gotcha, and it is the Part 5 ephemeral-ports callback. A NACL is stateless: it does not remember connections. If you allow an inbound packet, the response is not automatically allowed back — you must write a separate outbound rule for the return traffic. And because the return traffic goes to the client's ephemeral port (Part 5: the OS picks a high-numbered random source port, typically 1024–65535, for each outbound connection), your outbound rule must allow that whole ephemeral port range. Forget this and connections hang: the request gets in, the server replies, and the reply is silently dropped on the way out.

(2) Allow AND deny. Unlike security groups, NACLs support explicit deny rules. This is what makes them useful as a coarse blocklist: "deny all traffic from this known-bad IP range across the entire subnet," which an SG cannot express.

(3) Numbered and ordered — first match wins. NACL rules have rule numbers (e.g. 100, 200, 300). They are evaluated in ascending numeric order, and the first rule that matches a packet decides its fate — lower numbers win. (Security groups have no order; all rules are simply OR-ed together.) There is a final implicit * rule that denies anything unmatched.

The default NACL that comes with a VPC allows all inbound and all outbound traffic — so out of the box NACLs are wide open and the security groups do all the work. Most teams leave NACLs permissive and use them only as a targeted backstop.

🌍 Analogy — NACLs are the rules posted at the entrance to each floor

If security groups are bouncers at each room door checking badges, a NACL is the list of rules posted at the elevator lobby of an entire floor: "this floor admits these kinds of people and forbids those." It applies to everyone entering or leaving the floor, before they ever reach a room. And it is forgetful (stateless): it doesn't track who walked in, so if you want people to be able to walk back out, you must post a separate "exit allowed" rule — and exits leave through whatever door number they happened to use (the ephemeral ports). The floor rules can include an explicit "no entry for this group" (deny), which the room bouncers cannot. In practice the floor rules are usually "let everyone through" and the real control happens at the room doors.

If you start hand-managing NACL rules for normal app traffic, you will spend your life debugging the stateless ephemeral-port gotcha for little benefit.

🟢 Beginner — say it simply

A NACL is a firewall around a whole subnet (a floor), instead of one server. It's "forgetful": if you let a request in, you must separately let the reply out, and the reply leaves on a random high port number (Part 5). Unlike security groups, NACLs can explicitly block things. Most people leave NACLs open and rely on security groups.

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

NACLs are stateless, ordered (first-match), allow+deny L3/L4 filters at the subnet edge. Statelessness forces explicit return rules covering the client's ephemeral port range (Part 5). They cannot reference SGs (CIDR only). A packet must pass the subnet NACL and the resource SG. Default NACLs allow all; the recommended posture is permissive NACLs + tight SGs, with NACLs reserved for coarse subnet-wide deny.

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

Because NACLs are stateless and evaluated before SGs on ingress (and after on egress), they are useful for cheap, broad denials that you don't want hitting every instance's SG, and for compliance requirements that mandate subnet-level controls. The ephemeral range differs by OS (Linux 32768–60999, older Windows 49152–65535, NLB/Lambda may use others), so return rules typically allow 1024–65535 to be safe. NACLs apply to traffic crossing the subnet boundary, not to intra-subnet traffic — two instances in the same subnet are NACL-unfiltered between each other (only their SGs apply). Over-reliance on NACLs is an anti-pattern; they don't scale to application-identity policy the way SG references do.

🔑 Key point — this is why "it hangs" usually means NACL, and "refused" usually means SG

Hold onto this for the debugging section (§14). Because security groups are stateful, the failure mode you hit with a wrong NACL is a hang/timeout (the request got in but the stateless return was dropped, so the client waits forever — Part 4's "no RST, just silence"). A wrong security group, by contrast, tends to drop the packet before the connection establishes, which can also present as a timeout. Either way: a connection that times out rather than getting a clean refusal is almost always a firewall (SG or NACL) or routing problem, not your application code — because a closed port would send a TCP RST (Part 4), and a firewall silently swallows the packet instead.

🟢 Beginner — say it simply

A packet has to get past two checkpoints to reach your server: the floor rules (NACL) and the room bouncer (security group). On the way back, the security group remembers the conversation and lets the reply out automatically — but the NACL is forgetful, so you have to explicitly allow the reply. If a connection just hangs, a forgotten NACL return rule is a prime suspect.

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

Ingress order: IGW → subnet NACL (stateless) → resource SG (stateful). A packet must be allowed by both. On egress/return, SGs auto-allow established-flow replies; NACLs require explicit ephemeral-range outbound rules. The asymmetry means NACL misconfigurations manifest as one-directional hangs. Diagnose with this layered mental model rather than guessing.

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

The ALB breaks the flow into two independent TCP connections (client↔ALB and ALB↔target), so the app never sees the client's IP at L4 (it sees the ALB's) — the real client IP arrives in the X-Forwarded-For header (Part 10's reverse-proxy callback). This also means the app-tier SG/NACL rules are written against the ALB/VPC, not the public Internet. VPC Flow Logs (§12) record ACCEPT/REJECT at the ENI level and are the authoritative way to see which control dropped a packet — REJECT entries pinpoint an SG/NACL block, absence of any entry suggests a routing problem.

11. The ALB, finally placed inside the VPC

Part 9 taught you what a load balancer is (distributes traffic across healthy backends, does health checks, picks an algorithm) and Part 10 framed the ALB as a reverse proxy. Now we place it physically in the VPC, where all the pieces above give it a home.

ALB = OSI Layer 7 (Application) — routes on HTTP host/path. NLB = OSI Layer 4 (Transport) — routes on IP/port.

11.1 The ALB lives in the public subnets (one per AZ)

An ALB (Application Load Balancer) is the only Internet-facing component in our architecture. It requires a subnet in each Availability Zone it serves — that's why we built public subnets in both AZs (§4). AWS places ALB nodes in those subnets, each AZ's node getting an address there, and Route 53 returns the set so traffic spreads across AZs. The ALB has its own security group (sg-alb, §8) allowing 443 from the world, and it is the bridge between the public and private worlds: it is the only thing with a foot in the public subnet, and its target group points inward at your private app instances.

11.2 Target groups point at private-subnet app instances

A target group is the ALB's list of backends plus the health-check config (Part 9). The targets are your Spring Boot instances/tasks in the private app subnets, registered by instance ID or by IP, on port 8080. The ALB health-checks them (Part 9) and only routes to healthy ones; when one fails or an entire AZ goes dark, the ALB stops sending traffic there and your remaining AZ absorbs it. Because the app's security group allows 8080 only from sg-alb (§8), the ALB is literally the only thing that can reach the app — exactly the Part 10 principle that only the proxy is exposed.

11.3 NLB — the L4 sibling

An NLB (Network Load Balancer) does the same placement but operates at Layer 4 (Part 9): it forwards TCP/UDP by IP and port without understanding HTTP, handles extreme throughput and very low latency, and can have static IPs / Elastic IPs per AZ (useful when a client needs to allow-list a fixed IP, which an ALB's changing IPs make awkward). Use an ALB when you need HTTP-aware routing (host/path rules, header inspection — Part 10); use an NLB for raw TCP/UDP, ultra-low latency, or static IP requirements.

11.4 Route 53 ALIAS → the ALB

How does api.shop.example.com resolve to the ALB? This is the Parts 6 and 9 callback. An ALB doesn't have a fixed IP you can put in a DNS A record — its IPs change. So Route 53 (AWS's DNS, Part 6) provides an ALIAS record: a special record that points your domain at the ALB's AWS-managed DNS name and resolves, at query time, to the ALB's current IP addresses. ALIAS works at the zone apex (unlike a CNAME), is free to query, and tracks the ALB automatically.

🌍 Analogy — the ALB is the reception desk in the lobby

🟢 Beginner — say it simply

The load balancer (ALB) sits in the public subnets — it's the only thing the Internet can reach. It checks which of your private app servers are healthy and forwards each request to one of them. Your domain name points at the ALB through Route 53. The app servers themselves stay private and hidden.

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

An ALB requires a public subnet per AZ, has its own SG, terminates TLS (ACM cert), and forwards to a target group of private-subnet instances on the app port using L7 host/path rules and health checks (Part 9). It opens a separate backend connection, so the app sees the ALB's IP (real client in X-Forwarded-For, Part 10). Route 53 ALIAS maps the domain apex to the ALB's DNS name. NLB is the L4 alternative with static IPs and ultra-low latency.

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

"Internet-facing" vs "internal" is a scheme set at creation: internal ALBs live in private subnets and serve VPC-internal clients (Part 13's service-to-service). Cross-zone load balancing distributes across AZ targets evenly (on by default for ALB, off by default and chargeable for NLB). The ALB scales by adding nodes (its IPs change — hence ALIAS, never a hard-coded A record); targets registered by IP let it point at Fargate tasks and pods (Part 12). NLB preserves the client source IP to targets (unlike ALB) and supports TLS passthrough; choosing ALB vs NLB is an L7-features-vs-L4-rawness decision rooted in Part 9.

12. Other essentials you shouldn't be blindsided by

The core seven — VPC, subnet, route table, IGW, NAT, SG, NACL — plus the ALB are 90% of what you need. But a handful of other constructs appear constantly, so here is each one tightly, so nothing surprises you in a design review or Part 12.

12.1 RDS in private subnets

RDS (Relational Database Service) is managed PostgreSQL/MySQL/etc. It belongs in the private data subnets, reachable only via sg-db (§8). You give it a DB subnet group — a set of subnets across at least two AZs — so RDS can place the primary in one AZ and, with Multi-AZ enabled, a synchronous standby in another. On primary failure, RDS automatically fails over to the standby (DNS flips to the standby's address). This is the §2 multi-AZ availability principle applied to your database, and it's why a DB subnet group requires two AZs even for a single instance.

12.2 VPC Endpoints — reach AWS services without a NAT/IGW

Calling S3 or DynamoDB from a private subnet would normally go out through the NAT gateway and back — paying the data-processing charge (§7) and leaving the VPC. A VPC endpoint gives you a private path to AWS services that never touches the Internet:

  • Gateway endpoints (for S3 and DynamoDB only): add a route to your route table; free; traffic to S3/DynamoDB stays on the AWS network and skips the NAT gateway. This is the fix for the NAT bill surprise.
  • Interface endpoints (for most other services — Secrets Manager, ECR, SQS, etc.): an ENI with a private IP placed in your subnet, powered by PrivateLink; your app reaches the service at a private address. Hourly + per-GB cost, but keeps traffic private and off the IGW.

12.3 ENIs and secondary IPs (foreshadowing Part 12)

An ENI (Elastic Network Interface) is the virtual NIC inside the VPC — it holds a private IP (or several), security groups, and a MAC address. Everything in a VPC connects through an ENI. The reason to meet it now: an ENI can carry multiple secondary private IPs, and Part 12's EKS gives every Kubernetes pod its own VPC IP by attaching ENIs and handing out their secondary IPs to pods. That's why your app subnets must be sized for far more than your instance count — each pod is a first-class VPC citizen with a real 10.0.x.x address.

12.4 Elastic IP (EIP)

An EIP (Elastic IP) is a static public IPv4 address you own and can attach to an instance or NAT gateway. Unlike an auto-assigned public IP (which changes on stop/start, §6), an EIP stays put — useful for a NAT gateway's stable outbound address (so a partner can allow-list it) or any case needing a fixed public IP.

12.5 VPC Peering and Transit Gateway (VPC-to-VPC — the Part 1 WAN callback)

Two VPCs are isolated by default. To connect them: VPC peering is a direct 1:1 link between two VPCs (non-transitive — peering A↔B and B↔C does not connect A↔C, and their CIDRs must not overlap). For many VPCs, a TGW (Transit Gateway) is a hub-and-spoke router that interconnects dozens of VPCs (and on-prem) through one managed component. This is Part 1's WAN idea — connecting separate networks — done inside AWS between your own networks.

12.6 Site-to-Site VPN and Direct Connect (on-prem — the Part 1 WAN promise)

To connect your office or data center to the VPC (the WAN link Part 1 promised): Site-to-Site VPN is an encrypted IPsec tunnel over the public Internet (cheap, quick, subject to Internet latency/ jitter), and Direct Connect is a dedicated physical leased line into AWS (consistent latency, higher bandwidth, more cost and lead time). These are exactly Part 1's "lease capacity from carriers to connect sites" — VPN tunnels vs leased lines — now terminating in your VPC.

12.7 VPC Flow Logs — the debugging superpower

VPC Flow Logs record metadata for every IP flow at an ENI/subnet/VPC, including whether each flow was ACCEPTed or REJECTed. When a connection mysteriously times out (Part 4: RST vs timeout), Flow Logs answer the question "did the packet even arrive, and did an SG or NACL reject it?" A REJECT entry points straight at a firewall block; no entry at all points at routing. This is the difference between guessing and knowing in §14's debugging checklist.

🟢 Beginner — say it simply

Beyond the core pieces: your database (RDS) lives private and keeps a backup copy in another AZ; VPC endpoints let you reach AWS services like S3 privately and cheaply; Elastic IPs are fixed public addresses; peering and Transit Gateway connect VPCs together; VPN/Direct Connect connect your office to AWS; and Flow Logs are the camera that shows you what traffic was allowed or blocked.

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

RDS uses a multi-AZ DB subnet group for automatic failover. Gateway endpoints (S3/DynamoDB) and interface endpoints (PrivateLink, others) keep AWS-service traffic off the IGW/NAT. ENIs carry secondary IPs that EKS hands to pods (Part 12). EIPs are static public IPv4. Peering (1:1, non-transitive) and TGW (hub) connect VPCs; VPN (IPsec over Internet) and Direct Connect (leased line) connect on-prem. Flow Logs record per-flow ACCEPT/REJECT for forensics.

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

PrivateLink lets you expose your own service to other VPCs/accounts via an endpoint service (consumer-provider model, Part 13). Gateway endpoints inject managed-prefix-list routes; interface endpoints rely on private DNS overriding the public service name. ENI density and IP exhaustion drive EKS networking choices (prefix delegation, custom CNI). TGW supports route tables and segmentation for large multi-account landing zones; peering is cheaper but doesn't scale past a handful of VPCs. Flow Logs don't capture packet payloads (use traffic mirroring for that) and omit some traffic (link-local, DHCP); read them with the knowledge that a missing entry means the packet never reached the ENI — usually a routing or NACL issue upstream.

14. Cost and debugging wisdom — what actually bites you

Two practical disciplines separate engineers who merely deploy on AWS from those who run it well: knowing what costs money in the network, and knowing how to debug a connection that won't connect. Both lean entirely on Parts 1–10.

14.1 Data transfer cost: same-AZ vs cross-AZ vs Internet

Part 1 said distance has a price; AWS makes that literal in the bill. Roughly: traffic within one AZ between private IPs is free; traffic across AZs is charged per GB in both directions; traffic through the NAT gateway is charged per GB processed (§7); traffic out to the Internet is charged per GB egress. The lesson, which is just Part 1's latency advice with a dollar sign attached: keep chatty services co-located in the same AZ. Two microservices that exchange thousands of messages per request should be in the same AZ — you save both latency (~1 ms per cross-AZ round trip) and cross-AZ data charges. (HA still wants you spread across AZs; the art is balancing the two, usually by keeping a full stack in each AZ so the hot path stays local.)

14.2 The "connection timed out" debugging checklist

The single most common AWS networking incident is "my app can't connect to RDS / to another service, and it just times out." Part 4 gave you the crucial tell: a timeout (silence) almost never means the target app is down — a down app on a reachable host sends a TCP RST ("connection refused"), which is instant. Silence means a packet was swallowed somewhere — a firewall or a routing gap. So a timeout is almost always a networking misconfiguration, not your code. Walk this checklist in order:

"Connection to RDS at 10.0.20.x:5432 timed out" — debug in this order:

  [ ] 1. SECURITY GROUP (most common)
         Does sg-db allow inbound 5432 FROM sg-app (or the app's SG/CIDR)?
         Does sg-app allow OUTBOUND 5432 to sg-db?  (egress can be restricted)

  [ ] 2. SUBNET / ROUTE
         Is the app actually in the subnet you think? Same VPC as RDS?
         Does a route exist between the two subnets? (local route covers
         intra-VPC; peering/TGW needed across VPCs)

  [ ] 3. NACL
         Stateless! Does the data subnet's NACL allow inbound 5432
         AND outbound on the ephemeral range (1024-65535) for the return?

  [ ] 4. RIGHT TARGET
         Right endpoint/port? RDS on 5432, not the app's 8080?
         If Multi-AZ failover happened, are you using the RDS DNS name
         (which follows the failover) and not a hard-coded IP?

  [ ] 5. PUBLIC IP / REACHABILITY (for Internet-facing cases)
         Trying to reach something on the Internet from a private subnet?
         Is there a NAT route? For inbound, is there a public IP + IGW route?

  ──► still stuck? read VPC FLOW LOGS (§12): a REJECT names the SG/NACL block;
     NO entry at all means the packet never arrived — routing/wrong-subnet.
🔑 Key point — timeout vs refused is your first diagnostic fork

Memorize this fork from Part 4, because it instantly halves your search space: "connection refused" (RST) = you reached the host but nothing is listening on that port (app crashed, wrong port) — a software problem. "connection timed out" (silence) = a packet was dropped in flight (security group, NACL, route table, wrong subnet, no public IP) — a networking problem. In AWS the timeout case is overwhelmingly an SG that doesn't allow the traffic. Check the security group first, every time.

14.3 The placement principle: only expose the load balancer

The final piece of operational wisdom is a security principle that ties the whole chapter together and echoes Part 10: nothing should be public except the load balancer. App servers go in private subnets with no public IP; databases go in even more locked-down private subnets; the ALB in the public subnets is the single, narrow, HTTPS-only front door. The blast radius of a compromised app server is bounded because it can't be reached from the Internet and its egress is limited; the database is unreachable except from the app tier. Every "is this design secure?" review starts by checking that exactly one thing is internet-facing — and that one thing speaks only TLS.

🟢 Beginner — say it simply

Keep services that talk to each other a lot in the same data center (AZ) — it's faster and cheaper. When a connection "times out," it's almost always a firewall (security group) blocking it, not your code — a crashed app would say "connection refused" instantly. And only the load balancer should be reachable from the Internet; everything else stays private.

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

Cross-AZ and NAT/Internet data transfer cost money (Part 1's latency, priced); co-locate chatty paths, spread for HA. Diagnose connectivity with the timeout-vs-RST distinction (Part 4): timeout ⇒ SG/NACL/route/ subnet; refused ⇒ app/port. Run the SG → subnet/route → NACL → target → public-IP checklist, then confirm with Flow Logs. Architecturally, expose only the ALB; keep app and data private with SG-referencing-SG least privilege.

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

Cost-aware topology: same-AZ affinity (zone-aware clients, topology-aware routing in Part 13's mesh), gateway endpoints to dodge NAT charges, and CloudFront to cut egress. For debugging, Flow Logs + Reachability Analyzer (which statically traces whether a path is open and names the blocking component) turn guesswork into proof. The security posture generalizes to "assume breach": private subnets, least-privilege egress (so a compromised host can't exfiltrate freely), SG references over CIDRs (so policy survives scaling), and the ALB as the sole, WAF-frontable, TLS-only ingress — the cloud expression of Part 10's reverse-proxy isolation.

15. Interview questions

🎤 Try answering out loud before you click
What actually makes a subnet "public" in a VPC?

Its route table — specifically, a route sending 0.0.0.0/0 to an Internet Gateway. There is no "public" flag on a subnet; "public" and "private" are descriptions of where the default route points (IGW = public, NAT gateway or nothing = private). Full reachability also requires the instance to have a public IP or Elastic IP, since the IGW does 1:1 NAT between the private and public address. Strong answers state both conditions: the IGW route and a public IP, and note that the data tier often has no 0.0.0.0/0 route at all (fully isolated).

Security Group vs NACL — compare them precisely.

SGs operate at the ENI/instance level; NACLs at the subnet boundary. SGs are stateful (return traffic auto-allowed); NACLs are stateless (you must allow the return direction, including the client's ephemeral ports, Part 5). SGs are allow-only (implicit deny); NACLs support allow and deny. SG rules have no order (OR-ed); NACL rules are numbered and first-match. SGs can reference other SGs as source (identity-based); NACLs only take CIDRs. A packet must pass the NACL and the SG. Best practice: SGs as the primary fine-grained control, NACLs as a coarse backstop.

Why can a private instance reach the Internet but the Internet can't reach it?

Because of the NAT Gateway, which is the home-router NAT model (Part 3). The private instance's outbound packets go through the NAT gateway (sitting in a public subnet), which rewrites the source to its own Elastic IP and forwards via the IGW; return traffic for that established flow is allowed back. But there is no inbound mapping until the inside initiates, and the instance has no public IP, so nobody on the Internet can open a connection to it. Outbound-yes / inbound-no is the asymmetry of NAT — exactly how your home router protects your laptop.

How do you allow only the load balancer to reach your application servers?

Put a security group on the app (sg-app) whose inbound rule is "allow TCP 8080 from sg-alb" — the ALB's security group ID, not an IP range. This is SG-referencing-SG: only resources carrying the ALB's security group can reach 8080, and it keeps working as the ALB scales and changes IPs. The app servers have no public IP and live in a private subnet, so the ALB is literally the only thing that can connect to them. This three-tier SG chain (public→ALB, ALB→app, app→db) is the standard pattern.

Walk a request from the browser all the way to EC2 through the VPC.

(1) DNS: Route 53 ALIAS resolves api.shop.example.com to the ALB's IPs (Part 6). (2) Browser does TCP + TLS to an ALB public IP (Parts 4, 8). (3) The packet enters via the IGW into the public subnet, passing the subnet NACL and sg-alb (allow 443). (4) The ALB terminates TLS (ACM cert), picks a healthy target (Part 9), and opens a new connection to the app on 8080. (5) That enters the private app subnet, passing the NACL and sg-app (allow 8080 from sg-alb only). (6) Spring Boot queries RDS on 5432, allowed by sg-db (from sg-app). (7) Any external call egresses via the NAT gateway → IGW. (8) The response retraces, security groups auto-allow the returns (stateful), and the ALB re-encrypts to the browser. Naming the OSI layer and AWS control at each hop is what impresses.

Why deploy across multiple Availability Zones?

Because an AZ is a failure domain — it has independent power, cooling, and networking, and AWS designs for the possibility that a whole AZ fails. If all your capacity is in one AZ, that AZ's outage is your outage. Spreading app servers across 2–3 AZs (behind an ALB that health-checks and routes around failures) and using RDS Multi-AZ (synchronous standby in another AZ with automatic failover) means losing one AZ leaves a healthy copy serving traffic. It's the baseline shape of a correct deployment, not an add-on. The cost is some cross-AZ data transfer and latency, which you manage by keeping the hot path same-AZ where possible.

My app's connection to RDS is timing out. How do you debug it?

First, the tell: a timeout (not "connection refused") means a packet is being dropped, not that the DB is down — a down DB would RST instantly (Part 4). So it's a networking issue. Check in order: (1) Security group — does sg-db allow inbound 5432 from sg-app, and does sg-app allow that egress? (most common cause) (2) Subnet/route — same VPC, is there a route? (3) NACL — stateless, so does the data subnet allow inbound 5432 and outbound ephemeral ports for the return? (4) Right target/port and, after a Multi-AZ failover, are you using the RDS DNS name not a stale IP? (5) Reachability/public-IP for Internet cases. Then read VPC Flow Logs: a REJECT names the blocking SG/NACL; no entry means routing/wrong subnet.

What's the difference between an Internet Gateway and a NAT Gateway?

An IGW connects a VPC to the Internet and enables two-way reachability for instances that have public IPs (it does 1:1 NAT between private and public addresses); it's a redundant, managed, no-bandwidth-cap component, one per VPC. A NAT gateway gives private-subnet instances outbound-only Internet access (source NAT to its Elastic IP) and is itself unreachable for inbound from the Internet; it lives in a public subnet, is deployed one-per-AZ for HA, and costs an hourly + per-GB data-processing fee. In short: IGW = the door public servers use both ways; NAT GW = the one-way exit private servers use to reach out.

Are security groups stateful or stateless? Why does it matter?

Stateful. If you allow an inbound connection, the reply is automatically allowed out (and vice versa) because the SG tracks each connection's state (Part 4). This means you never write return rules — allow inbound 443 and the response just flows. It matters because NACLs are the opposite (stateless): with a NACL you must explicitly allow the return traffic on the client's ephemeral port range (Part 5), and forgetting that is the classic cause of connections that hang. The statefulness is also why SG misconfigurations and NACL misconfigurations present differently when debugging.

Can two security groups reference each other, and why would you?

Yes. A rule's source/destination can be another SG's ID, and groups can reference each other (e.g. app↔ cache). You do it for identity-based microsegmentation: instead of allow-listing IPs (which change as instances scale), you say "allow from anything carrying sg-app." The rule survives autoscaling, instance replacement, and IP churn because AWS resolves the group to its current members in the data plane. It's the cleanest way to express "these tiers may talk" without maintaining IP lists, and it's the backbone of the three-tier SG chain.

Where does a VPC live — one AZ, or the whole Region? And a subnet?

A VPC is Region-wide: a single VPC spans all the AZs in its Region. A subnet, by contrast, lives in exactly one AZ and never spans two. That's why high-availability designs create one subnet per tier per AZ (mirrored pairs): the VPC is the Region-wide container, and you place per-AZ subnets inside it. A common misconception is that a VPC is tied to one AZ — it isn't; the per-AZ unit is the subnet.

Does AWS use special networking, or real TCP/IP? And where does the ALB sit (L4 or L7)?

Real, ordinary TCP/IP. AWS virtualized the routers, switches, and firewalls into software (SDN), but the packets are standard IP packets carrying standard TCP segments — everything from Parts 1–8 applies unchanged. As for the ALB: it's a Layer 7 load balancer (it understands HTTP host/path and can route on them, terminate TLS, inject headers — Parts 9, 10). The NLB is the Layer 4 sibling, forwarding TCP/UDP by IP/port with ultra-low latency and static IPs. Security groups and NACLs filter at L3/L4 (IP and port); route tables forward at L3. Mapping each AWS construct to its OSI layer is the mental model that makes the whole system legible.

16. Common misconceptions

❌ Myths engineers actually believe

❌ "A subnet is public because of a setting on the subnet."
✅ There is no "public" flag. A subnet is public only because its route table sends 0.0.0.0/0 to an Internet Gateway (and the instance has a public IP for the IGW to NAT). Change that route to a NAT gateway and the same subnet becomes private. "Public" describes the route table, full stop.

❌ "Security groups can block or deny traffic."
✅ Security groups are allow-only; there is no deny rule. To block something with an SG, you simply don't allow it (implicit deny). Explicit deny lives in NACLs, which is what you use to blocklist a known-bad IP range across a subnet. If you're trying to "deny" in an SG, you're looking at the wrong tool.

❌ "A NAT gateway makes my instances reachable from the Internet."
✅ The opposite. A NAT gateway provides outbound-only access for private instances and is itself unreachable for inbound from the Internet — exactly like your home router. To accept inbound, you need an Internet Gateway route plus a public IP (and in practice you front everything with the ALB instead). NAT = a one-way exit.

❌ "AWS uses some special networking, not real TCP/IP."
✅ It's ordinary TCP/IP. AWS virtualized routers, switches, and firewalls into software-defined networking, but the packets are standard IP packets carrying standard TCP segments. Everything you learned in Parts 1–8 applies unchanged inside a VPC — the CIDR math, the handshake, the ports, the TLS. The "cloud" part is the configuration model, not the protocols.

❌ "A VPC lives in one Availability Zone."
✅ A VPC spans all AZs in its Region. The per-AZ unit is the subnet — each subnet is pinned to exactly one AZ. That's precisely why HA designs put one subnet per tier in each AZ inside the single, Region-wide VPC. Confusing the VPC's scope with the subnet's scope is a very common error.

❌ "I need a public IP on my application servers."
✅ You almost never do. App servers belong in private subnets with no public IP, behind the ALB. The ALB is the only internet-facing component; it forwards to your private instances on the app port. Giving app servers public IPs widens the attack surface for no benefit — the load balancer is your front door, and the NAT gateway handles any outbound they need.

❌ "Security groups are stateless like NACLs, so I must allow return traffic."
✅ Security groups are stateful — return traffic for an allowed connection is permitted automatically, so you never write return rules. It's NACLs that are stateless and require explicit return rules on the ephemeral port range (Part 5). Swapping these two in your head is the root of both "why won't this connect?" and "why did I open ports I didn't need to?"

❌ "All my outbound traffic is basically free / the NAT gateway is cheap."
✅ The NAT gateway charges an hourly fee plus a per-GB data-processing fee on everything through it, and cross-AZ traffic is billed per GB too — a notorious source of surprise bills. Route S3/DynamoDB through free gateway VPC endpoints, keep chatty traffic same-AZ, and use CloudFront to cut Internet egress. Network cost is Part 1's "distance has a price," made literal.
