TCP & UDP — Moving Data Reliably (or Fast)

Part 3 got your packet to the right machine — and that is everything IP will ever do for you. Packets get lost, duplicated, reordered, and corrupted, and IP never apologizes. This part is about the two protocols that decide what to do about that: TCP, which manufactures a reliable byte pipe out of unreliable parts, and UDP, which honestly refuses to. Every connection your backend has ever opened — to PostgreSQL, Redis, Kafka, another microservice — is one of these two, and most of your weirdest production bugs live in this chapter.

1. The gap Part 3 leaves: IP is best-effort

Part 3 ended on what looked like victory: a packet addressed to 203.0.113.10 left your laptop, got NAT-translated at your router, hopped through a dozen routers it had never met, and arrived at the right network interface on the right machine, anywhere on Earth. Genuinely one of the great engineering feats of the 20th century. Now read the fine print of the contract that made it possible:

🔑 Key point

IP (Internet Protocol) offers best-effort delivery. It will try to deliver each packet. It promises nothing else: not arrival, not arrival once, not arrival in order, not arrival intact — and crucially, it never tells the sender what happened. No receipt, no error report, no apology. "Best effort" is the politest possible name for "no guarantees whatsoever."

This is not sloppiness — each failure mode falls straight out of how Parts 1–3 said the Internet works:

  • Loss. Routers have finite queues; when traffic arrives faster than a link drains, the router throws packets away — legal, routine, by design. Radio links (Wi-Fi, 4G) add loss from noise.
  • Reordering. Each packet routes independently (Part 3); if the path changes mid-conversation, packet 7 can overtake packet 6.
  • Duplication. Retransmission logic plus routing quirks can deliver the same packet twice.
  • Corruption. Noise flips bits; Layer 2 checksums (Part 2) discard most damaged frames — turning corruption into loss — and the few that slip through are why TCP and UDP carry their own checksums.

🧭 Why a transport layer exists

No application you have ever written can live on that contract directly. An INSERT INTO orders… where the middle third of the SQL evaporates, a JSON response with its chunks shuffled, a Kafka message delivered twice because the network felt like it — these are not edge cases an app can shrug off. Applications need a different promise: "the bytes I send arrive exactly once, in order, intact — or I find out that they could not." Since routers refuse to make that promise (and we'll see why they're right to refuse), something at the endpoints must manufacture reliability out of unreliable parts. That something is the transport layer — Layer 4 — and its two famous citizens, TCP and UDP, are the subject of this entire part.

📜 What came before

The ARPANET (the Internet's ancestor — Part 1) originally ran NCP (Network Control Program), which simply assumed the network was reliable — reasonably, because the ARPANET's routers (IMPs, Interface Message Processors) did hop-by-hop acknowledgment internally. That worked on one homogeneous network and died as an idea the moment people wanted to interconnect different networks — noisy packet radio, half-second satellite links, lossy early Ethernets. You cannot demand every network on Earth be reliable inside. In May 1974, Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn published "A Protocol for Packet Network Intercommunication": one protocol (just called TCP) letting unreliable networks interconnect by pushing reliability to the hosts. By 1978 it was split in two — IP, the dumb best-effort forwarding layer any network could implement, and TCP, the reliability layer running only at the endpoints. On 1 January 1983 — Part 1's "flag day" — the ARPANET switched off NCP and the TCP/IP era began. UDP was carved out in 1980 (RFC 768) for apps wanting raw datagram service with no reliability machinery at all.

1.1 The end-to-end principle: dumb network, smart edges

That 1978 split embodies a design philosophy with a name — the end-to-end principle, formalized in a famous 1984 paper by Saltzer, Reed, and Clark. The argument: features like reliability should live at the ends of a communication path, not in the middle, because (a) only the endpoints know what the application actually needs — retransmit? how patiently? in what order?; (b) even a perfectly reliable middle can't save you — if a router acknowledges your packet and then crashes, hop-by-hop reliability guaranteed nothing end to end; and (c) per-conversation bookkeeping for millions of flows would make routers slow, expensive, and fragile. So the Internet's core stays dumb and stateless — routers forward packets and forget them — while intelligence lives at the edges, in the operating systems of the two endpoint machines.

This is why the OSI badge on this page matters (Part 2 callback): a router is a Layer 3 device — it reads the frame and IP headers and stops parsing. Every mechanism in this chapter runs in exactly two places: your kernel and the server's. The 14 routers in between have no idea a "connection" exists (Part 3's NAT is the famous cheat — it peeks into Layer 4 to rewrite ports). The payoff: the core stays simple enough to scale to billions of devices, and transports can evolve at the edges — exactly how QUIC (end of this part) later deployed a brand-new transport onto the existing Internet with zero router upgrades.

🌍 Analogy — the postal system makes the same deal

The post office (Part 1's running analogy) is best-effort too: ordinary letters occasionally vanish, arrive out of order, or show up twice, and no mail truck driver tracks your correspondence. If you need certainty, the postal system doesn't change — you upgrade at the edges: you send registered mail with numbered receipts, and you, the sender, keep a ledger and re-send anything unacknowledged. The trucks stay dumb; the sender and recipient get smart. Hold that image: TCP is exactly "registered mail with numbered receipts," implemented by the two endpoints over the same dumb trucks. UDP, coming up first, is the postcard: cheap, instant, and entirely unaccounted for.

2. UDP — the thinnest possible protocol

We start with UDP (User Datagram Protocol) not because it's less important but because it's simpler — so simple that describing it takes one section, and everything afterward is TCP earning its complexity.

🧭 Why this exists

Without UDP, every application would be forced through TCP's full machinery: connections, acknowledgments, retransmission, ordering. For some applications that machinery is wasted weight or actively harmful. A DNS lookup is one tiny question and answer — a connection setup would triple its cost. A retransmitted live-voice packet arrives after the moment it described — reliability delivering a paperweight. So in 1980, David Reed's UDP (RFC 768) became the escape hatch: hand a single datagram to IP with almost nothing added, and let the application decide what reliability even means for its data.

UDP adds exactly four fields — eight bytes — on top of IP:

   bit 0               15 16              31

   ├───────────────────┼───────────────────┤
   │      length       │     checksum      │   header + data length /
   │     (16 bits)     │     (16 bits)     │   corruption detection

Two of those fields answer Part 1's "which app?" problem (problem #6 of the ten). An IP address gets a packet to a machine — but the machine runs Spring Boot, PostgreSQL, an SSH daemon, and forty browser tabs. A port is a 16-bit number (0–65535) naming which program's mailbox the data belongs to: PostgreSQL listens on 5432, Redis on 6379, Tomcat on 8080; the source port is the return address. That gentle definition is all we need today — Part 5 is entirely about ports and sockets, including where source ports come from and what a socket actually is.

Now the defining feature of UDP — the list of things it does not do:

  • No connection. There is no handshake, no setup, no teardown, no "session." You fling a datagram at an address and port. The first packet can carry data.
  • No delivery guarantee. Lost is lost. UDP doesn't know, doesn't retry, doesn't tell you.
  • No ordering. Datagrams arrive in whatever order the network produces.
  • No duplicate suppression. Delivered twice? You get it twice.
  • No flow control, no congestion control. UDP will cheerfully let you firehose a receiver or a congested network as fast as your NIC can transmit. (The checksum — detecting corruption — is the one service it does perform, and even that was optional in IPv4.)

Each datagram is independent, like a postcard: self-addressed, self-contained, unacknowledged. One useful consequence: UDP does preserve message boundaries. One send() = one datagram = one receive() at the other end. Hold that thought — TCP, surprisingly, will not give you that.

🌍 Analogy — postcards

UDP is the postcard tier of the postal system: cheap to send, no envelope, no receipt, no tracking number. Drop fifty postcards in the box and they may arrive shuffled, and number 37 may never arrive at all — and the postal service is not lying to you, because it never claimed otherwise. When is a postcard the right choice? When the message is tiny, when speed matters more than certainty, or when the information goes stale so fast that re-sending a lost one would be pointless ("wish you were here" three weeks after you got home). Keep that last idea: late data is useless data is the single best predictor that a system should use UDP.

2.1 Who actually uses UDP — and why it's the right call

  • DNS (Domain Name System — Part 6). One question ("what's the IP for api.example.com?"), one answer, each fitting in a single datagram — a handshake would triple the round trips. The resolver handles reliability itself (no answer soon? ask again, or ask another server) and falls back to TCP for oversized responses (Part 6).
  • Live voice and video (Zoom, WebRTC). If frame 412 is lost, stalling frames 413–500 to retransmit it is the worst response — that moment of the call is already gone. Drop it, interpolate, move on: TCP's reliability is actively harmful here.
  • Multiplayer games. Only the latest position matters; retransmitting where a player was 200 ms ago is worse than useless. Game netcode sends frequent small UDP updates and shrugs at loss.
  • Metrics fire-and-forget. StatsD emits counters over UDP (port 8125) so metrics can never slow or break the hot path; losing 0.1% of increments under load is a fine trade for zero added latency.
  • QUIC. The modern twist: QUIC (the transport under HTTP/3) rebuilds TCP-grade reliability, ordering, and congestion control in userspace, over UDP. Why not improve TCP itself? TCP lives in kernels and middleboxes that take decades to upgrade; UDP passes through everything today. Parts 7–8 pick this story up.

🟢 Beginner — say it simply

UDP is "send and hope": you throw a small packet at an address and port, with no setup and no receipt. It's perfect when the message is tiny (DNS) or goes stale instantly (live video, game positions), because waiting to re-send a lost packet would be slower and more annoying than just losing it.

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

UDP adds ports and a checksum to IP — nothing else. No connection state, no ordering, no retransmission, no flow/congestion control; datagram boundaries are preserved 1:1. Applications that choose UDP take over reliability themselves where needed: DNS retries with transaction IDs, video uses jitter buffers and FEC (forward error correction), QUIC implements full reliability in userspace.

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

UDP's statelessness is an operational superpower and hazard at once: servers can answer enormous query volumes with no per-client memory (DNS roots), but no handshake means source addresses are trivially spoofed — the root of reflection/amplification DDoS attacks, and the reason UDP services need their own anti-spoofing (DNS cookies, QUIC's address validation). Also note kernel buffers: a burst of datagrams that outruns net.core.rmem_max is silently dropped on your own box — the first place to look when StatsD metrics go missing under load.

3. TCP — the contract

🧭 Why this exists

Almost everything a backend does — SQL queries, HTTP APIs, cache reads, message publishing — needs the opposite of a postcard: every byte arrives, exactly once, in order, or the application is told the connection failed. Building that per-application on best-effort IP would mean every developer re-implementing acknowledgment ledgers, timers, retransmission, and reordering buffers — badly, with subtle bugs in each. TCP (Transmission Control Protocol) implements it once, in the kernel, behind the world's most successful abstraction: a plain, reliable, two-way pipe of bytes. Without it, the web and databases as you know them would not exist in recognizable form.

TCP's contract has six clauses. Each one is a mechanism we will open up in the sections that follow:

  1. Reliable — every byte is accounted for; lost data is detected and retransmitted, and if the network is truly broken, you get an error instead of silence (§9).
  2. Ordered — bytes are delivered to the application in exactly the order they were written, even if packets arrived shuffled (§5).
  3. Byte-stream — TCP moves a continuous stream of bytes with no message boundaries — the most underrated fact in this course (§4).
  4. Connection-oriented — before data flows, both ends establish shared state via a handshake (§6), and tear it down explicitly (§7).
  5. Flow-controlled — a fast sender cannot drown a slow receiver (§10).
  6. Congestion-controlled — a sender backs off when the network is overloaded (§11).

All six clauses run on one toolkit: number every byte, acknowledge what arrives, time and resend what doesn't, buffer at both ends. TCP's data unit is the segment (Part 2's vocabulary: a segment rides inside an IP packet inside a link-layer frame). Here is the header that runs the show — every field pays for one of the six promises:

TCP is the registered-mail tier of our postal system, run entirely by the two correspondents. Every page you mail carries a serial number (sequence numbers); the recipient mails back receipts — "I now hold every page up to #4500" (cumulative ACKs); you keep copies and re-mail anything unconfirmed for too long (retransmission). The correspondence opens by exchanging starting serial numbers (the handshake) and ends with formal goodbye letters in both directions (FIN). The trucks underneath are the same lossy, reordering, best-effort trucks as ever — all the certainty is manufactured by the two of you, at the edges. That is TCP in one paragraph; the rest of this part is the engineering inside it.

4. A stream of bytes, not messages

Before the handshake and the state machines, one concept — because it is the TCP fact most likely to bite you in application code this year. TCP has no idea what a "message" is. It accepts bytes from your app, pools them, slices them into segments wherever it finds convenient, and delivers an identical byte sequence on the far side. How your writes were grouped is not recorded, not transmitted, and not reconstructable.

Here it is in code you can run tonight. The sender calls write() twice; how many data events does the receiver get?

// sender.js — the app makes TWO writes
const net = require("net");
const sock = net.connect(9000, "10.0.1.20", function () {
  sock.write("HELLO");     // write #1 — 5 bytes
  sock.write("WORLD");     // write #2 — 5 bytes
});

// receiver.js — how many 'data' events? NO GUARANTEE WHATSOEVER.
const net = require("net");
net.createServer(function (sock) {
  sock.on("data", function (chunk) {
    console.log("got", chunk.length, "bytes:", chunk.toString());
  });
}).listen(9000);

// on localhost you will usually see ONE event:
//     got 10 bytes: HELLOWORLD
// across a real network, under load, you might see:
//     got 7 bytes: HELLOWO
//     got 3 bytes: RLD
// and any other split is equally legal.

The classic career moment: code that parses "one data event = one JSON message" works perfectly on localhost (where writes usually arrive 1:1), survives the demo, then corrupts data in production the first time a message straddles two segments. Java devs hit the same wall expecting one InputStream.read() to return exactly one logical message.

🔑 Key point

Because TCP refuses to mark message boundaries, every application protocol on top of TCP must invent its own framing — a rule for where messages start and end. The three classic designs: length prefixes — "the next 4 bytes say how long the message is" (Kafka's wire protocol and PostgreSQL's both do exactly this); delimiters — "a message ends at CRLF" (Redis's RESP protocol, HTTP/1.1 header lines); and declared length in a header — HTTP's Content-Length, which Part 7 will show riding on everything you learn today. When you write a custom TCP protocol and forget framing, you haven't written a protocol — you've written a localhost demo.

4.1 MSS vs MTU — how big is a segment?

How big is a slice? Part 2 introduced the MTU (Maximum Transmission Unit) — the largest IP packet a link will carry, classically 1500 bytes for Ethernet. Subtract a 20-byte IP header and a 20-byte TCP header: 1460 bytes of payload. That is the MSS (Maximum Segment Size) — the most application data one segment can carry. Each side announces its MSS in the handshake (watch for mss 1460 in §6's tcpdump) and the smaller wins; pre-slicing to fit the MTU avoids IP fragmentation (Part 3's slow, fragile fallback). A 1 MB API response therefore leaves as roughly 720 segments.

🟢 Beginner — say it simply

TCP is a conveyor belt of bytes, not a parcel service. You pour bytes in; the same bytes pour out the other end in order — but your neat packages get melted together. If your app needs "messages," it must draw the lines itself, e.g. "first 4 bytes = length of what follows."

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

The kernel coalesces and splits writes into segments bounded by MSS (MTU − IP header − TCP header = 1500 − 40 = 1460 on plain Ethernet), influenced by the window (§10), Nagle (§12), and retransmission. Reads return whatever contiguous bytes the receive buffer holds. Robust TCP code always loops: accumulate bytes, repeatedly try to parse complete frames, keep the remainder.

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

Real stacks complicate the picture further: TSO/GSO offload lets the kernel hand the NIC 64 KB "supersegments" that the hardware slices, and GRO re-merges on receive — so even tcpdump's view differs from the wire. Path MTU discovery adjusts effective MSS when a tunnel (VPN, VXLAN in Kubernetes — Part 12) shrinks the MTU below 1500; when ICMP "fragmentation needed" messages are firewalled away, you get the infamous PMTU black hole: small requests work, big responses hang. If "curl works but the file download stalls at 1460 bytes," you are living this bug.

5. Sequence numbers and ACKs — the bookkeeping of certainty

Now open the ledger. TCP numbers bytes, not segments. Each direction of the connection picks a starting number — the ISN (Initial Sequence Number) — and from then on every byte in that direction has a position: ISN+1, ISN+2, ISN+3… A segment's sequence number field holds the stream position of its first payload byte; a segment carrying bytes 4001–4500 has seq=4001 and length 500.

Acknowledgment runs on one elegant convention — cumulative ACKs:

🔑 Key point

The acknowledgment number means: "I have received every byte up to here; this is the next byte I expect." ACK 1001 = "everything through byte 1000 is safely mine; send 1001 next." One number summarizes the entire history of the conversation — which makes ACKs cheap, and makes a lost ACK usually harmless, because any later ACK re-confirms everything before it.

Both directions run independently: each side numbers its own outgoing bytes and acknowledges the other's. And because every TCP segment has both a sequence field and an ACK field, acknowledgments usually piggyback on data flowing the other way — a response segment carries new data and confirms your request in one packet. Here's a complete worked example: your Spring Boot app sends a 500-byte request; PostgreSQL replies with 2,400 bytes (which exceeds one 1460-byte MSS, so it ships as two segments). Client ISN = 4000, server ISN = 9000, so first data bytes are 4001 and 9001:

Had an earlier ACK been lost, the final one still confirms everything — cumulative ACKs are self-healing. Lost data, and the ledger that stops adding up, is §9's story. But first: where do those ISNs come from? Each side must learn the other's starting number before any bookkeeping can work — which is exactly what the handshake is for.

6. The three-way handshake

🧭 Why this exists

TCP's machinery requires shared state: each side must know the other's ISN (or no ACK arithmetic works), agree on options (MSS, SACK, window scaling), and allocate buffers and a control block in the kernel. None of that exists for a new peer — so before the first data byte, both sides must synchronize. The handshake is that synchronization, and it doubles as proof of life: each side demonstrates it can both send to and receive from the other. Without it, you could ACK nothing, order nothing, retransmit nothing — every promise in §3 depends on these three packets.

Why exactly three? Count what must be proven. A connection needs both ISNs delivered and acknowledged — x must reach the server and be confirmed back; y must reach the client and be confirmed back. That is four logical messages: SYN(x), ACK(x), SYN(y), ACK(y). But the middle two travel the same direction at the same moment, so TCP merges them into one packet — the SYN-ACK — giving three. Two packets cannot work: after just SYN and SYN-ACK, the server has no evidence its y ever arrived — it would be running a connection on an unconfirmed number, and a connection request from a long-delayed duplicate SYN (from a connection that no longer exists) would be indistinguishable from a real one. Three is not a convention; it is the provable minimum. Note also the +1s: a SYN consumes one sequence number even though it carries no data — that's why the ACKs say x+1 and y+1, and it's how the handshake itself gets reliability (an unACKed SYN is retransmitted like any other byte).

6.1 Why ISNs are randomized

If every connection started at sequence 0, two ugly things happen. Security: an off-path attacker who can guess your sequence numbers can forge segments that land inside your window — injecting an RST to kill a connection (a real attack against long-lived BGP router sessions) or even injecting data; predictable ISNs powered the famous 1990s IP-spoofing attacks, so modern stacks (RFC 6528) derive the ISN from a keyed hash of the connection's addresses and ports plus a clock. Correctness: address/port pairs get reused over time, and a delayed packet from the previous connection could wander into the new one — widely-spaced random ISNs make it fall outside the valid window and be discarded instead of accepted as fresh data. (TIME_WAIT in §7 is the other half of this defense.)

6.3 The handshake tax — and why connection pooling exists

The handshake costs one full RTT before the first useful byte (Part 1: RTT — round-trip time — is the distance tax physics charges). On a LAN, sub-millisecond and ignorable. Cross-region, it is not — and it's only the start, because a fresh database connection stacks more round trips on top: TLS negotiation (Part 8) and PostgreSQL's own startup and authentication exchange. The arithmetic for one freshly-opened connection per request:

This is the reason connection pooling exists. HikariCP is not a Java optimization — it is a TCP optimization: pay the connection tax once per pooled connection at startup, then amortize it across millions of queries. The same physics explains HTTP keep-alive (one TCP connection, many requests — Part 7) and why Kafka and Redis clients hold sockets open for hours. 🌍 Analogy — the handshake is exchanging receipt books

Before our registered-mail correspondence begins: "I'd like to correspond; my receipt book starts at number 2,837,514,871." — "Received; mine starts at 901,442,310." — "Received." Three letters, no fewer, and both ledgers are synchronized. And if the correspondence will span many exchanges, you can see why we keep the channel open (pooling) rather than re-exchanging receipt books before every single letter.

🟢 Beginner — say it simply

Before talking, both computers swap "hello" notes: client says hello with a starting number, server says "got it, here's mine," client says "got yours." Three messages, one round trip, and now both sides can track every byte. Opening connections is expensive over distance, so backends open them once and reuse them — that is all a connection pool is.

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

SYN(seq=x) → SYN-ACK(seq=y, ack=x+1) → ACK(ack=y+1); both ISNs exchanged AND acknowledged, which two packets cannot achieve. SYN/FIN each consume one sequence number. Options (MSS, SACK-permitted, window scale, timestamps) are negotiated only here — they cannot be added later. Total cost: 1 RTT, which compounds with TLS and application auth into the "connection tax" that pooling and keep-alive amortize.

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

The client may send data with packet 3 (zero extra cost), and TCP Fast Open (TFO) even allows data on the SYN for repeat connections via a server-issued cookie — though middleboxes mangled TFO badly enough that it never conquered the public Internet; that lesson ("you can't upgrade TCP in place") is a direct cause of QUIC-over-UDP, which folds transport and crypto setup into a single round trip (and zero for resumption). Also know: connect() returning success only proves the handshake finished — the server process may still be wedged; that's why health checks speak the application protocol, not just TCP (Part 9).

7.2 Closing: the four-way teardown and half-close

7.3 TIME_WAIT — the misunderstood hero

After sending the final ACK (packet 4), the active closer — whoever sent the first FIN — does not go straight to CLOSED. It parks in TIME_WAIT for 2 × MSL (Maximum Segment Lifetime — the longest a segment is presumed able to wander the network; the spec says MSL = 2 minutes, so 4 minutes of waiting; Linux hardcodes the whole wait to 60 seconds). Two jobs justify the wait:

  1. The final ACK might be lost. If it is, the server — stuck in LAST_ACK — retransmits its FIN. Someone must still be at the address to re-ACK it, or the server's clean shutdown turns into an error:

8. RST — what error messages really mean

FIN is the polite goodbye. RST (reset) is the slammed door: "there is no such conversation — stop immediately." A kernel sends RST when a segment arrives for a connection it has no record of: a SYN to a port nobody is listening on, data for a connection that died with its process, anything after an abort. No ceremony, no TIME_WAIT — and unlike FIN, an RST can mean in-flight data was discarded. 9. Retransmission — how loss actually gets repaired

Back to the ledger from §5. The sender keeps a copy of every transmitted-but-unACKed byte. Two separate tripwires decide when a copy gets re-sent.

9.1 Tripwire one: the retransmission timer (RTO)

For its oldest unACKed data the sender runs a timer — the RTO (Retransmission TimeOut). If it fires before an ACK covers that data, the sender assumes loss and retransmits. The timer must adapt — 1 ms is absurd across an ocean, 1 s absurd within a rack — so TCP continuously measures its own segments' RTT and maintains a smoothed RTT estimate (SRTT) plus a variance estimate: RTO ≈ SRTT + 4 × variance (the Jacobson/Karels algorithm), floored at 200 ms on Linux, starting at 1 s before any measurement exists. Each consecutive failure doubles the RTO — the same exponential backoff SYNs used in Fig 4.10, so a dead peer is probed gently rather than hammered.

10. Flow control — protecting the receiver

🧭 Why this exists

Reliability creates a new problem. The receiver's kernel buffers arriving bytes until the application reads them — and applications stall: GC (garbage collection) pauses, slow disks, a phone on a weak signal. A fast sender transmitting into a full buffer forces drops — which TCP's rules would dutifully retransmit into the same full buffer, forever. The fix: the receiver continuously tells the sender how much room it has, and the sender never exceeds it. That is flow control.

The mechanism is the window field you saw in the header (§3): in every segment it sends, the receiver advertises its receive window — rwnd — the number of bytes of buffer space it currently has free. The sender may have at most that many bytes in flight (sent but unACKed) at any moment. Visualized over the sender's outgoing stream, this is the famous sliding window:

            the sender's view of its own outgoing byte stream

   │  ACKed       │  yet ACKed    │  (room left in  │  yet (beyond     │
   │  (retired)   │  (in flight)  │   the window)   │   the window)    │
   └──────────────┴───────────────┴─────────────────┴──────────────────┘

                  └── window starts at the          └── window ends at
                      oldest unACKed byte               start + window size

   every arriving ACK slides the whole window to the right: bytes
   retire on the left, new bytes become sendable on the right.
   in-flight bytes never exceed the advertised window.

The beautiful edge case: a completely full receiver advertises rwnd = 0 — the zero window — and the sender must stop entirely, no matter how much data is queued. Picture PostgreSQL mid-stream while your app sits in a GC pause: the receive buffer fills, win 0 goes out, and the database falls silent. To avoid deadlock (the "window reopened" ACK could itself be lost), the sender keeps sending tiny persist probes — "any room yet?" — with backoff, until an ACK finally advertises space and data flows again.

🧪 Real example — you have used flow control without knowing it

Streaming a huge PostgreSQL result. You stream a 10-million-row ResultSet with a small fetchSize, processing slowly: your receive buffer fills, rwnd hits zero, and PostgreSQL itself stops sending — backpressure propagates from your while (rs.next()) loop into the database's network stack, no code required. A slow HTTP client on 2G downloading your 40 MB report throttles Tomcat with its rwnd; the servlet thread blocks inside outputStream.write() — one way slow clients pin server threads (Part 9's buffering proxies rescue this). Node makes it visible: sock.write() returning false means the pipe is full; the drain event is the window reopening. Node's stream backpressure API is TCP flow control surfacing into JavaScript.

10.1 Window scaling — when 64 KB stopped being enough

The window field is 16 bits: maximum 65,535 bytes — generous in 1981, crippling now. Recall the BDP (bandwidth-delay product) from Part 1: the pipe holds bandwidth × RTT. A 1 Gbps path at 100 ms RTT holds 125,000,000 bytes/s × 0.1 s = 12.5 MB in flight — but a sender capped at 64 KB unACKed transmits 64 KB then idles a full RTT for ACKs: 65,535 ÷ 0.1 s ≈ 5.2 Mbps. On a gigabit link. That is why the handshake negotiated wscale 7: the window scale option multiplies the advertised window by 2^7 = 128 (shifts up to 14 allow windows near 1 GB). Negotiable only in the SYN exchange — one more thing a connection cannot change after birth.

11. Congestion control — protecting the network

📜 What came before — the day the Internet choked

Original TCP had flow control only: it protected receivers and trusted the network to cope. In October 1986 it stopped coping: the link between Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory and UC Berkeley — 400 meters, three hops — fell from 32 kbps to 40 bits per second, a thousandfold collapse. The death spiral: overloaded routers dropped packets; every sender's RTO fired and retransmitted; the retransmissions deepened the overload, causing more drops, causing more retransmissions — until the network's capacity was spent carrying copies of data it was about to discard. This was congestion collapse. Van Jacobson and Michael Karels diagnosed it and in 1988 retrofitted the cure into TCP: treat the network as a shared resource, probe its capacity, back off when it signals distress. Every TCP connection on Earth still runs their medicine.

The cure introduces a second window, this one maintained privately by the sender: the congestion window — cwnd — the sender's running estimate of how much data the network path can absorb. Nobody advertises it; the sender infers it from evidence. And it joins rwnd in one beautifully simple rule:

🔑 Key point

A sender may have at most min(rwnd, cwnd) bytes in flight. Two protectors, one limit: rwnd protects the receiver (it says so explicitly, in every ACK); cwnd protects the network (the sender guesses, watching for loss). Your effective throughput is whichever guardian is currently stricter — when a transfer is slow, the first diagnostic question is which window is pinching.

How does the sender grow and shrink cwnd? Two phases, both visible in the sawtooth below:

  • Slow start (misleading name — it's exponential). A new connection starts cwnd at about 10 segments (~14.6 KB on modern Linux) and doubles it every RTT — each ACK earns more credit — probing upward fast until loss occurs or a threshold (ssthresh) is crossed.
  • Congestion avoidance — AIMD (Additive Increase, Multiplicative Decrease). Past the threshold, growth turns gentle: roughly +1 MSS per RTT (additive). On loss, cwnd is halved (multiplicative). Climb slowly, fall hard — AIMD is mathematically what makes many competing flows converge toward fair shares instead of starving each other.
  • Loss is the signal. A router with a full queue drops your packet; your dup-ACKs or RTO detect it; you interpret it as "the network is full" and retreat. Packet loss on the Internet is not (only) failure — it is the network's language for "slow down," and TCP deliberately drives into it to find the ceiling. (Modern extras like ECN — Explicit Congestion Notification, §3 — let routers mark instead of drop, and delay-based algorithms listen to rising RTT instead.)

Two modern algorithms to know by name. CUBIC — the Linux default since 2006, i.e. what your EC2 instances and containers run — replaces AIMD's straight climb with a cubic curve: recover fast toward the previous ceiling, plateau cautiously near it, then probe beyond; far better on high-BDP paths. High throughput, low queueing delay ("bufferbloat" relief), resilient on lossy links; YouTube runs on it, and on Linux it's one sysctl away (net.ipv4.tcp_congestion_control=bbr).

🔑 Key point — cold connections are slow connections

Slow start means a fresh connection's first round trips move tiny amounts: ~14.6 KB, then ~29 KB, then ~58 KB… A 100 KB response on a brand-new connection pays three or four RTTs of ramp-up that a warm connection (with a grown cwnd) would not. The full cold bill: handshake (§6) + TLS (Part 8) + slow-start ramp — a pooled connection pays none of it. The third independent reason pooling and keep-alive win, and why HTTP/2 multiplexes requests over one warm connection rather than opening six cold ones (Part 7).

🟢 Beginner — say it simply

Two brakes keep TCP polite. The receiver says "I only have room for this much" (flow control). The sender also watches for lost packets — the network's way of saying "I'm overloaded" — and slows itself down (congestion control). New connections start cautiously and speed up, like merging onto a highway, which is why reusing warm connections is faster than opening new ones.

🟡 Intermediate — the mechanics

In-flight data ≤ min(rwnd, cwnd). rwnd: receiver-advertised, scales via the handshake-negotiated window scale option; zero-window stalls are real backpressure (slow consumers, slow clients). cwnd: slow start doubles per RTT from ~10 segments, then AIMD (+1 MSS/RTT, halve on loss); dup-ACKs trigger fast retransmit, RTO is the fallback. Linux default CUBIC; BBR paces from a bandwidth/RTT model instead of loss.

🔴 Advanced — what experts know

Throughput on lossy paths follows rate ≈ MSS ÷ (RTT × √loss): a 0.1% loss rate quietly caps a 100 ms flow far below link speed — why cross-region replication (PostgreSQL, Kafka MirrorMaker) is so sensitive to tiny loss. Idle pooled connections may have cwnd decayed by the kernel (tcp_slow_start_after_idle; latency-sensitive shops disable it). Bufferbloat — oversized queues delaying the loss signal — is BBR's reason to exist. And cwnd is per-connection: 8 parallel connections grab ~8 shares, which is why download managers "work" and polite services don't do that.

12. Nagle's algorithm and delayed ACK — the 40 ms mystery

📜 What came before

1984, the era of telnet: every keystroke an interactive session sent became its own segment — 1 byte of payload wearing 40 bytes of TCP/IP headers, a 4000% overhead, multiplied across every typist on a 56 kbps line. John Nagle's RFC 896 proposed the obvious economy: coalesce tiny writes. The rule — while a small segment is in flight unACKed, buffer further small writes and send them together once the ACK returns (or a full MSS accumulates). Independently, receivers adopted delayed ACK (RFC 1122): on receiving data, wait up to ~40 ms before ACKing, hoping to piggyback the ACK on response data or cover several segments with one ACK. Each idea is sensible alone. Together they interlock into one of networking's most infamous performance bugs.

The trap springs on a write–write–read pattern — say a client that writes a small header, then a small body, then waits for the response:

The cure is the socket option TCP_NODELAY — disable Nagle, send small segments immediately. You already benefit from it unknowingly: Redis clients (Jedis, Lettuce) set it — a cache answering in 0.2 ms cannot tolerate 40 ms stalls; gRPC's Netty transport sets it; game servers set it reflexively. In your own code: socket.setTcpNoDelay(true) in Java, socket.setNoDelay(true) in Node. Modern frameworks pair it with application-level buffering — assemble the full message, write once — keeping Nagle's economy without its latency. Nagle still helps genuinely interactive byte-dribbles (its telnet habitat) and apps spraying tiny writes across a WAN; the backend default is: complete messages, single writes, TCP_NODELAY on.

13. The two keep-alives — same name, different universes

🔑 Key point — disambiguate or be confused forever

TCP keepalive and HTTP Keep-Alive share a name and nothing else. TCP keepalive is an OS-level liveness probe: with SO_KEEPALIVE enabled, the kernel sends tiny probe segments on an idle connection — by default starting after two hours — to check the peer still exists, and kills the connection after enough silence. HTTP Keep-Alive is an application-level reuse convention: send multiple HTTP requests over one TCP connection instead of opening a fresh one per request (Part 7's territory — it's how HTTP dodges §6's handshake tax). One probes for death; the other amortizes birth. Saying "keep-alive" in a design review without qualifying which one is a small act of sabotage.

The OS-level defaults are visible (and tunable) on any Linux box:

$ sysctl net.ipv4.tcp_keepalive_time net.ipv4.tcp_keepalive_intvl net.ipv4.tcp_keepalive_probes
net.ipv4.tcp_keepalive_time = 7200     # idle seconds before the FIRST probe (2 hours!)
net.ipv4.tcp_keepalive_intvl = 75      # seconds between probes
net.ipv4.tcp_keepalive_probes = 9      # unanswered probes before declaring death

# 7200 + 9 × 75 ≈ 2 hours 11 minutes before a dead peer is noticed —
# and even this only applies if the app opted in with SO_KEEPALIVE
# (the PostgreSQL JDBC driver does, with tcpKeepAlive=true)

13.1 Why idle connections die anyway

The operational punchline: an idle TCP connection generates zero packets, and per the end-to-end principle (§1) only the endpoints know it exists — but the stateful middleboxes on the path (Part 3's NAT, Part 9's load balancers, corporate firewalls) keep per-connection table entries and evict idle ones to reclaim memory. AWS's NAT Gateway forgets idle flows at 350 seconds; an Application Load Balancer kills idle connections at 60 seconds by default (Part 9 dwells on this). Both endpoints still believe the connection is ESTABLISHED — ss swears it on both machines! — but the path has amnesia: the next write vanishes into futile retransmission (a timeout minutes later) or draws an instant RST. That is the anatomy of the classic "first request after lunch fails, the retry works" bug: the pool handed your code a connection that died quietly during the quiet hour.

🧪 Real example — how mature pools defend themselves

Every serious connection-pool feature you've configured is a workaround for silent connection death:

                                                  # younger than most middlebox quirks

Same idea everywhere in your stack: Redis clients ping idle connections; Kafka clients recycle sockets idle longer than connections.max.idle.ms (default 9 minutes); the PostgreSQL JDBC driver exposes tcpKeepAlive=true. The pattern to remember: either traffic flows through a connection regularly, or something must actively keep it alive — or it will die, silently, and you'll meet it as a weird timeout at the worst time.

14. SYN floods and the accept backlog

One last look at the handshake, this time from the defender's chair. Between packets 1 and 3 (Fig 4.7) the server holds a half-open connection — state allocated for a peer that has proven nothing yet. The kernel parks these in a SYN queue; when the final ACK lands, the connection graduates to a second queue — the accept queue — where it waits for the application to claim it with accept(). Two queues, two distinct ways to suffer:

Attack: the SYN flood. An attacker sprays SYNs from spoofed sources and never completes a handshake. Each one squats in the SYN queue until its timeout; the queue fills; legitimate SYNs get dropped — service down, at trivial attacker cost. The 1996-era classic, still launched daily. The elegant defense: SYN cookies. When the SYN queue overflows, the kernel stops storing state and instead encodes the would-be connection's essentials (a timestamp, an MSS class) into the cryptographic hash it uses as the ISN of its SYN-ACK — then forgets the client entirely. A legitimate client's final ACK must echo that ISN + 1 (§5's arithmetic!), so the server can validate the cookie and rebuild the connection from the ACK itself — zero memory spent on liars. Trade-off: options the cookie can't encode are lost, so cookies engage only under attack.

Self-inflicted: accept-queue overflow. No attacker required — just your own success. If your app accepts slower than connections arrive (a long GC pause, all threads busy, a wedged event loop), the accept queue hits its cap and the kernel drops new handshakes — clients see timeouts and resets while your process is technically up. The cap is listen()'s backlog argument, bounded by net.core.somaxconn (historically 128; 4096 on modern kernels) — surfaced as Tomcat's acceptCount (default 100), Netty's SO_BACKLOG, and the third argument to Node's server.listen() (default 511).

Bigger backlogs buy burst absorption; only faster accept-and-work — or more replicas (Part 9) — buys real capacity.

The decision guidance for a backend engineer, compressed:

  • Default to TCP. If the data must be complete and correct — API calls, queries, messages, files — the choice is already made, and made well.
  • Choose UDP when late data is worthless data — live media, game state, real-time telemetry — because retransmission would deliver the past.
  • Choose UDP for tiny one-shot exchanges with app-level retry — DNS-shaped problems where a handshake would triple the cost.
  • Choose UDP when you're implementing your own transport — you are QUIC, a game-netcode author, or similarly serious; you now owe the network congestion control, and the burden of §9–§11 is yours.
  • Audit your own stack: Tomcat/Spring Boot on 8080 — TCP. PostgreSQL 5432 — TCP. Redis 6379 — TCP. Kafka 9092 — TCP. SSH 22 — TCP. The exceptions you actually run: every DNS lookup your services make (UDP 53, TCP fallback — Part 6), StatsD metrics (UDP 8125), and — increasingly — HTTP/3 to your CDN edge, which is QUIC over UDP 443 (Parts 7–8 tell that story).

One closing synthesis: "TCP vs UDP" is really kernel transport vs userspace transport. Choosing UDP for anything nontrivial means re-implementing loss detection, ordering, and congestion control yourself — which is why serious UDP systems converge on QUIC rather than raw datagrams, and why everything else in your stack happily stays on TCP.

16. Interview questions

🎤 Try answering out loud before you click
Walk me through the TCP three-way handshake. What is actually being exchanged?

Client sends SYN with its randomized Initial Sequence Number x (plus options: MSS, SACK-permitted, window scale, timestamps). Server replies SYN-ACK: its own ISN y, with ack=x+1. Client replies ACK with ack=y+1. Both sides now hold each other's starting byte numbers — the foundation for all sequence/ACK arithmetic — have negotiated options (settable only here), allocated kernel state, and proven they can both send and receive. Cost: one full RTT before the first data byte (data may ride on the third packet). Strong answers note SYN consumes one sequence number — hence the +1 — and can sketch the tcpdump flags [S], [S.], [.].

Why three packets — why not two, and why not four?

A connection requires both ISNs delivered and acknowledged — four logical messages: SYN(x), ACK(x), SYN(y), ACK(y). The server's ACK of x and its own SYN(y) travel the same direction at the same moment, so TCP coalesces them into one SYN-ACK: four messages, three packets. Four packets would work but wastes one. Two cannot work: after just SYN + SYN-ACK, the server has no evidence its ISN y ever arrived — it would commit state for a possibly-absent peer, unable to distinguish a genuine client from a delayed duplicate SYN of a dead connection. Each side must have its number explicitly confirmed; three packets is the provable minimum.

What is TIME_WAIT for? Is a large number of TIME_WAIT sockets a problem?

TIME_WAIT is held by the active closer (whoever sent the first FIN) for 2×MSL — Maximum Segment Lifetime; 60 seconds total on Linux. Two purposes: (1) if the final ACK is lost, the peer retransmits its FIN from LAST_ACK, and someone must still exist to re-ACK it — otherwise the peer gets an RST for a clean close; (2) it quarantines the 4-tuple until stray delayed segments from the old connection have expired, so they can't be misread as data on a new connection reusing it. Many TIME_WAITs are normal — a sign of churning short-lived connections, not a leak (each expires on schedule). If counts threaten ephemeral-port exhaustion, the fix is pooling/keep-alive, not sysctl folklore.

Explain flow control vs congestion control.

Both throttle the sender; they protect different victims. Flow control protects the receiver: it advertises its free buffer (rwnd) in every ACK's window field and the sender never exceeds it — down to a full stop on a zero window, with persist probes to detect reopening. Congestion control protects the network: the sender maintains a private congestion window (cwnd), grown by slow start (doubling per RTT) then AIMD (+1 MSS per RTT, halved on loss), treating loss as the network's "slow down" signal — the lesson of the 1986 collapse. The true limit is min(rwnd, cwnd): explicit information from the receiver, inference about the network. Bonus: name CUBIC (Linux default) and BBR (model-based pacing).

"Connection refused" vs "connection timed out" — what does each tell you?

Refused: your SYN reached a live machine whose OS answered with RST — nothing is listening on that port. Fast (one RTT) and rich in information: routing, NAT, and firewalls all passed; suspect the service (down, wrong port, bound to 127.0.0.1, container port unmapped). Timed out: your SYNs were silently dropped — a firewall or AWS Security Group (which drop, never reject), a black-hole route, a dead host — and the client retried with exponential backoff for ~2 minutes. Slow and ambiguous. Java: ConnectException: Connection refused vs SocketTimeoutException; Node: ECONNREFUSED vs ETIMEDOUT. Mid-stream ECONNRESET is the third sibling: an established connection destroyed — peer crash, reboot, or middlebox idle-timeout.

Why do we pool database connections? Answer in terms of TCP.

Because connection establishment is round-trip-expensive and pooling amortizes it to zero. A fresh PostgreSQL connection pays the TCP handshake (1 RTT), the TLS handshake (1–2 RTTs), and the database's startup/authentication exchange (several RTTs) — at 70 ms cross-region RTT, roughly 400 ms before the first query. Two further TCP reasons: a fresh connection starts in slow start with a tiny congestion window, so early transfers are slow until cwnd grows; and churning connections piles up TIME_WAIT and burns ephemeral ports. HikariCP, HTTP keep-alive, and long-lived Kafka/Redis connections are the same physics: pay the connection tax once, reuse forever, validate idle connections so middleboxes haven't silently killed them.

When would you choose UDP over TCP?

Three honest cases. (1) Late data is worthless: live voice/video, game state, real-time telemetry — retransmission delivers the past, so skip losses and stay current; TCP's reliability is actively harmful here. (2) Tiny one-shot exchanges where a handshake would dominate and the app can retry: DNS (with TCP fallback for big responses); StatsD metrics accept loss to keep the hot path non-blocking. (3) You are building your own transport — QUIC being the proof it's possible and the warning of how much work it is. Everything else — APIs, databases, queues, files — defaults to TCP. Strong answers add: raw UDP at scale must implement congestion control or it's a bad citizen and a DDoS-amplification risk.

What does "TCP is a byte stream" mean practically for application code?

TCP transmits an ordered byte sequence with no record of how writes were grouped: two write() calls may arrive as one read(), or one write as five reads — only content and order are preserved. Code assuming "one data event = one message" works on localhost and corrupts in production. So every TCP application protocol defines framing: length prefixes (Kafka, PostgreSQL), delimiters (Redis RESP's CRLF, HTTP/1.1 header lines), or declared sizes (HTTP's Content-Length). Robust readers loop: buffer, extract complete frames, retain the remainder. Noting that UDP preserves datagram boundaries — one of the few things it does "better" — completes the answer.

How does TCP recover a lost segment? Compare the two paths.

Path one — RTO (Retransmission TimeOut): the sender times its oldest unACKed data against an adaptive timeout derived from smoothed RTT plus variance (initial 1 s; 200 ms floor on Linux); expiry triggers retransmission, and the RTO doubles per consecutive failure. Correct but costly — a full RTO of dead air, which is what wrecks p99 latency. Path two — fast retransmit: out-of-order arrivals make the receiver repeat its last cumulative ACK; three duplicate ACKs read as "exactly one segment died while later ones survived," so the sender retransmits immediately. With SACK (negotiated at handshake) the receiver reports precisely which ranges it holds, so only true holes are resent. After recovery the cumulative ACK leaps forward over everything buffered out of order.

What is a SYN flood, and how do SYN cookies defeat it?

A SYN flood exploits the handshake's asymmetry: each spoofed SYN makes the server allocate a half-open entry in its SYN queue while the attacker spends nothing and never completes step three. The queue fills; legitimate SYNs are dropped. SYN cookies remove the memory cost: under pressure, the kernel encodes the connection essentials (timestamp, MSS class) into a keyed hash used as the ISN of its SYN-ACK, then stores nothing. A genuine client's final ACK must echo that ISN+1, letting the server validate and reconstruct state from the ACK alone — attackers' half-opens now cost zero bytes. Trade-off: options the cookie can't encode are lost, so cookies activate only on overflow. Related and self-inflicted: accept-queue overflow (app accepting too slowly — GC pauses) drops handshakes with no attacker; check ss -lnt and the listen-overflow counters.

You see thousands of sockets in CLOSE_WAIT on your service. What happened?

The peers closed; your application didn't. CLOSE_WAIT means the remote FIN arrived and was ACKed by your kernel, which now waits — indefinitely — for your process to call close(). A pileup is always an application-level socket leak: HTTP response bodies never closed, connections opened outside the pool, exception paths skipping cleanup. The fix is guaranteed-cleanup constructs (Java try-with-resources, Node ensuring destroy()/end() on error paths) plus leak detection (HikariCP's leakDetectionThreshold). Contrast for the interviewer: TIME_WAIT piles indicate connection churn (fix: pooling) and self-expire in 60 s; CLOSE_WAIT piles indicate a bug and persist until the process restarts.

A service makes two small writes then reads the reply, and every call takes ~40 ms longer than expected. Diagnose it.

The Nagle/delayed-ACK interaction. Nagle (RFC 896) holds back small writes while an earlier small segment is unACKed, to coalesce telnet-era tinygrams; delayed ACK (RFC 1122) makes the receiver postpone its ACK up to ~40 ms hoping to piggyback it on response data. Write–write–read interlocks them: the sender holds the second write awaiting an ACK; the receiver holds the ACK awaiting a response it can't form without that write — a mutual-politeness stall broken only by the delayed-ACK timer. Fixes, best first: write each logical message in one write; enable TCP_NODELAY (setTcpNoDelay(true) in Java, setNoDelay(true) in Node) as Redis clients and gRPC/Netty already do. Recognizing a ~40 ms quantum in latency histograms as this signature is senior-engineer pattern matching.

17. Common misconceptions

❌ Myths engineers actually believe

❌ "TCP guarantees delivery."
✅ Nothing can guarantee delivery over a cuttable cable — if the path is truly gone, no protocol saves you. TCP guarantees detection and honest accounting: every byte is tracked, lost data is retransmitted with adaptive timeouts, and if the network stays broken, you get an explicit error instead of silent loss. The contract is "delivered exactly once, in order — or you find out." Detect-and-retry-or-fail, not magic.

❌ "UDP is always faster in a way that matters."
✅ Packets travel the same wires at the same speed under both protocols. UDP genuinely saves the handshake RTT, slow-start ramp, and retransmission stalls — decisive for one-shot lookups and live media, irrelevant for a warm, pooled TCP connection in steady state. And if your data must be complete, you'll rebuild TCP's machinery on UDP yourself — usually slower and buggier.

❌ "The TCP handshake exchanges encryption keys."
✅ The TCP handshake exchanges sequence numbers and options — nothing secret, everything in cleartext (you read one in tcpdump in §6). Encryption is TLS (Transport Layer Security), a separate handshake that runs after TCP's, with its own round trips and certificates — that's Part 8. The layering is literal: TCP connect, then TLS handshake, then HTTP. Three different handshakes, three different jobs.

❌ "TCP sends my messages."
✅ TCP sends a byte stream. Your three writes may arrive as one read; your one write as five. No message boundaries survive the trip — framing (length prefixes, delimiters, Content-Length) is the application protocol's job, which is precisely why HTTP, Kafka, PostgreSQL, and Redis each define one. Code that assumes one-write-one-read is a localhost demo with a production incident in its future.

❌ "HTTP keep-alive and TCP keepalive are the same thing."
✅ TCP keepalive = kernel-level liveness probes on idle connections (default: first probe after two hours, opt-in via SO_KEEPALIVE). HTTP Keep-Alive = application-level connection reuse — many requests per TCP connection, dodging the handshake tax (Part 7). One detects death; the other amortizes birth. They share six letters and zero mechanism.

❌ "All these TIME_WAIT sockets are a leak/bug — let's tune them away."
✅ TIME_WAIT is a correctness feature: the active closer lingers 2×MSL to re-ACK retransmitted FINs and to let stale segments die before the address pair is reused. Entries self-expire (60 s on Linux). Thousands of them are a signal — you're churning short connections — and the cure is pooling/keep-alive, not registry spells. The genuinely scary state is CLOSE_WAIT, which never expires and always means your code leaked a socket.

❌ "A TCP connection is a thing that exists in the network."
✅ A connection is two blobs of kernel state at the endpoints — sequence numbers, buffers, timers — and nothing in between (§1's end-to-end principle; routers stop reading at Layer 3). Its packets may even take different routes. This is why idle connections "die" when a NAT or load balancer forgets its table entry: there was never a circuit to keep alive, only two optimistic endpoints and some middleboxes with memory.
